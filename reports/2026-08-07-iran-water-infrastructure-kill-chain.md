## Bradlee — So, what's the answer?

Yes — but only in fragments. The Nexus's own daily reports touched this story three separate times (2026-07-23, 2026-07-29, 2026-08-06) without ever connecting them, and the 07-29 report explicitly flagged the biggest incident — 30+ Minnesota water systems — as unattributed, "worth watching, not yet established." This report is the first place the full arc lives in one document.

The full arc is bigger than any single Nexus report suggested. It starts in November 2023, when CyberAv3ngers — a group tied to Iran's IRGC Cyber-Electronic Command — compromised the Municipal Water Authority of Aliquippa, PA, exploiting Unitronics PLCs with no password or a default one. The US Treasury sanctioned six named IRGC-CEC officials for it in February 2024. That exact playbook resurfaced in 2026: a CISA/FBI/EPA/NSA/DOE advisory (AA26-097A) opened April 7, warning of the same actor cluster hitting internet-exposed Rockwell PLCs; it was updated July 22 to add Schneider Electric and Siemens devices after the campaign "recently escalated, likely in response to hostilities between Iran, and the United States and Israel." Four days later, July 26–27, more than 30 municipal water systems across Minnesota were hit in a coordinated attack. By early August the count had grown to at least 12 states, including a confirmed water-pressure drop and boil-water advisory at Clayton County, Georgia's utility (300,000 customers). Forescout's independent Aug 6 scan found 4,407 internet-exposed Rockwell controllers globally, 65% in the US, with 22 sitting in cities already hit.

**Is it Iran?** Probably, but not confirmed, and that distinction has become a live political fight, not just a technical one. US intelligence agencies' preliminary assessment — "likely" Iran, consistent with CyberAv3ngers tradecraft — was explicitly and publicly contradicted by President Trump on July 31, who blamed Minnesota's "grossly incompetent" state government instead; Governor Walz shot back that Trump "knows exactly who is responsible." No federal agency has issued a formal attribution as of this report. Investigators are also reportedly weighing whether the campaign could be a different actor imitating Iran's known tradecraft during active US-Iran hostilities — a real possibility given how low-skill the underlying technique is (default credentials on exposed devices, no new vulnerability required), though no source found in this research offers it as a competing hypothesis with its own evidence, only as a process caveat.

**The kill chain holds up better than most.** Unlike a targeted campaign, this is opportunistic exploitation at scale — Weaponization, Delivery, and Installation in the classic sense barely apply, because there's no crafted lure or bespoke malware; the exposure itself is the entry point. What CISA's own advisory documents cleanly is Initial Access (internet-exposed PLCs, weak credentials) → Command and Control (the device's own OT ports serve as the channel, plus Dropbear SSH on compromised modems) → Exfiltration (legitimate vendor software pulls PLC project files to actor infrastructure) → Impact (logic modification, HMI/SCADA manipulation, and — at least once — deliberate disabling of safety shutdown and alarm logic). That last detail matters: this isn't purely a psychological-operations story. Real safety controls were removed from at least one live US system.

**What this actually means:** the campaign's value to whoever is running it looks less like a single dramatic act and more like ongoing, low-cost reconnaissance-and-disruption at scale, against a sector (small municipal water utilities) that is structurally unable to defend itself quickly. Nothing here has yet caused confirmed harm to drinking water. Whether that holds is the report's central open question — see Seldon's forecast below for a range, not a guess.

## Sherlock — What are the facts?

**Actor.** CyberAv3ngers, aka Shahid Kaveh Group, Hydro Kitten, Storm-0784, APT Iran, Bauxite, Mr. Soul, Soldiers of Solomon, and UNC5691 — a threat group affiliated with Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Cyber-Electronic Command (IRGC-CEC). This is CISA's own list of aliases for the actor cluster it attributes the historical (2023) activity to; the agencies stop short of formally attributing the 2026 campaign to the same group by name, describing it only as "a group of Iranian-affiliated APT actors."

**Historical baseline (Tier 1 — Treasury's own sanctions record, independently corroborated by contemporaneous reporting).** In November 2023, CyberAv3ngers compromised at least 75 Unitronics Vision-series PLCs across the US, Israel, UK, and Ireland — devices that had no password or used a default one. At the Municipal Water Authority of Aliquippa, PA, the group defaced an HMI display with "You have been hacked, down with Israel" and shut down a water pumping station; no water-safety compromise resulted. In February 2024, the US Treasury sanctioned six named IRGC-CEC officials (Hamid Homayunfal, Hamid Reza Lashgarian, Mahdi Lashgarian, Milad Mansuri, Mohammad Amin Saberian, Mohammad Bagher Shirinkar) under Executive Order 13224 — the first time the US government publicly tied a hacktivist persona directly to named IRGC military officials. The State Department separately offered a $10 million Rewards for Justice bounty for information on the group.

**The 2026 campaign (Tier 1 for technical detail — CISA's joint advisory, independently corroborated by Forescout's own scan; Tier 2-and-weaker for the Iran attribution specifically — see below).**

- **April 7, 2026** — CISA, FBI, NSA, EPA, DOE, US Cyber Command's Cyber National Mission Force, and Treasury jointly publish Advisory AA26-097A, warning of ongoing Iranian-affiliated targeting of internet-connected PLCs, initially scoped to Rockwell Automation/Allen-Bradley devices, across Water and Wastewater Systems, Energy, and Government Services/local-municipality sectors.
- **Since at least March 2026** — the authoring agencies, through direct victim engagements, identify an Iranian-affiliated APT group disrupting PLC function at organizations across those sectors; some victims experience operational disruption and financial loss.
- **July 22, 2026** — the advisory is updated: manufacturer scope expands to Schneider Electric (BMX P34/Modicon M340) and Siemens (S7-1200 series) PLCs alongside Rockwell (CompactLogix, Micro850); new guidance is added on detecting malicious changes to reusable logic modules (Add-On Instructions); the agencies state the campaign has "recently escalated, likely in response to hostilities between Iran, and the United States and Israel." At one US victim, the FBI observed a malicious project file that retained normal ladder logic but added code overriding the instructions responsible for maintaining safe operating parameters.
- **July 26–27, 2026** — a coordinated cyberattack hits more than 30 municipal water and wastewater systems across Minnesota. Plymouth, South St. Paul, Maple Plain, and Braham publicly confirm operational technology (control valves, pumps) was affected, forcing manual operation. No water-quality or supply compromise is reported.
- **July 28, 2026** — Minnesota IT Services publicly discloses the attack statewide.
- **July 29–30, 2026** — The Register, Washington Post, and NBC News report that US intelligence agencies' preliminary assessment names Iran, consistent with CyberAv3ngers tradecraft, as the likely actor — explicitly caveated by sources as unconfirmed and subject to change, with investigators reportedly also weighing whether the incident could be a different actor imitating Iran amid active US-Iran hostilities.
- **July 30, 2026** — FBI and EPA jointly report water/wastewater incidents across at least seven states over a four-day period, describing "loss of monitoring and control functionality."
- **July 31, 2026** — President Trump publicly rejects the Iran attribution in cabinet remarks, blaming Minnesota's state government — "grossly incompetent," pointing to "the corrupt governor of Minnesota" — rather than Iran. Governor Tim Walz responds: "Trump knows exactly who is responsible for this attack, and knows that other states were hit too. This is what modern warfare looks like, and it further illustrates there's no plan to win a war with Iran." No technical counter-evidence for the Minnesota-culpability claim has been made public by the administration.
- **August 3, 2026** — The Register reports Georgia and Michigan have also confirmed water-system hacks tied to the Iran-linked actor cluster.
- **Early August 2026** — Clayton County Water Authority (Georgia, serving roughly 300,000 people in the Atlanta area) experiences cyber activity causing a water-pressure drop and a boil-water advisory; service is restored within hours.
- **August 5–6, 2026** — Bloomberg reports the campaign continuing; CBS News reports at least 12 states now affected (naming Michigan, Minnesota, Georgia, New Jersey, South Dakota; Wisconsin is separately named by other outlets). Forescout publishes an independent technical analysis (Shodan-based scan, snapshot August 3): 4,407 internet-facing Rockwell/Allen-Bradley controllers exposed on port 44818 (EtherNet/IP) worldwide, 65% in the US; device-model breakdown MicroLogix 1400 (50%), CompactLogix 1769 (22%), MicroLogix 1100 (8%), ControlLogix 5590 (8%); over 70% of exposed US devices reachable via cellular modem; 22 of the exposed hosts sit specifically in cities already hit by the campaign, 86% of which run firmware vulnerable to CVE-2017-16740 (though exploitation of that specific flaw is not confirmed — the attackers' method is credential abuse, not new-vulnerability exploitation). Attack method: remote IP-address and password changes to lock legitimate operators out of monitoring and control, plus direct PLC logic modification observed at least once. Reported impacts at some sites include pressure loss and flooding risk that could allow untreated groundwater into drinking-water pipes.
- **As of this report** — no US federal agency has issued a formal, named attribution to Iran or to CyberAv3ngers specifically. The characterization throughout US reporting remains "likely," "consistent with," or "suspected" — sourced to anonymous officials, not an on-record agency statement.

### The Kill Chain, Phase by Phase

CISA's advisory maps cleanly to four of the seven classic Lockheed Martin phases; the other three don't meaningfully apply to opportunistic, scale-based exploitation of already-exposed devices rather than a deliberately targeted campaign (see Euclid, below, for why).

**1. Initial Access — Tier 1.** Actors use several foreign-based IP addresses, from leased third-party hosted infrastructure, to access internet-facing PLCs manufactured by Rockwell Automation/Allen-Bradley, Schneider Electric, and Siemens [MITRE ATT&CK for ICS **T0883**, Internet Accessible Device]. Inbound malicious traffic targets PLC ports **44818, 2222, 102, and 502**, and modems on port **22**. No new vulnerability is exploited — CISA states explicitly this advisory "is not highlighting a new vulnerability in the identified products, but instead discusses opportunistic targeting" of devices with insufficient network and hardening controls.

**2. Weaponization — doesn't meaningfully apply.** There is no bespoke malware payload built in advance. The actors' "weapon" is legitimate vendor configuration software, repurposed.

**3. Delivery — doesn't meaningfully apply.** There is no phishing lure or crafted attachment delivered to a person. The exposure itself — a misconfigured, internet-reachable device — is the vector; nothing is "delivered" to it beyond the actor's own connection.

**4. Exploitation — Tier 1.** No software vulnerability is exploited; the actors log in using weak or default credentials, the same pattern documented at Aliquippa in 2023, and Forescout's independent scan corroborates the scale of exposure this depends on.

**5. Installation — Tier 2, partial.** In one reported instance, actors installed Dropbear SSH software on a victim's cellular modem to enable persistent remote access through port 22 [**T1219**, Remote Access Tools]. On the PLC itself, "installation" takes the form of malicious ladder logic embedded directly in the device's project file rather than a separate malware binary — CISA notes this pattern, first seen in 2023, "continues to be observed to date."

**6. Command and Control — Tier 1.** The compromised device's own OT management ports serve as the C2 channel [**T0885**, Commonly Used Port] — there is no separate C2 infrastructure in the conventional sense. CISA's advisory publishes a specific IOC list of IP addresses tied to this actor cluster, with active-use windows running from September 2025 through July 2026.

**7. Actions on Objectives — Tier 1 for the technique, Tier 2 for specific victim outcomes.** Actors use legitimate vendor configuration software (Rockwell's Studio 5000 Logix Designer, Schneider's EcoStruxure Control Expert, Siemens' TIA Portal), run on leased third-party infrastructure, to exfiltrate device project files [**T1041**, Exfiltration Over C2 Channel]. After extraction, CISA and FBI identified modification and deletion of project-file logic — including Add-On Instructions — and manipulation of data displayed on HMI and SCADA screens [**T1565**, Data Manipulation]. Critically, at one US victim, these changes disabled safety shutdown and alarm logic, letting the system enter an unsafe state without warning the operator. Named victim-level outcomes: 30+ Minnesota systems forced to manual operation (July 26–27); Clayton County, GA's water-pressure drop and boil-water advisory (early August); pressure loss and flooding risk reported at some of the 22 identified targeted-city hosts.

## Euclid — What must be fundamentally true?

The Lockheed Martin kill chain assumes a human adversary deliberately choosing a target and building a chain of distinct actions to reach it. That assumption doesn't hold here, and the mismatch is structural, not a gap in this report's research. What CISA describes is scale-based opportunism: actors (or, plausibly, scanning tooling working on their behalf) sweep for internet-exposed PLCs across a known set of OT ports, and whichever devices turn out to have weak or default credentials become targets — not because they were chosen, but because they were findable. That's why three of the seven phases (Weaponization, Delivery, Installation-in-the-classic-sense) don't cleanly apply: there's no crafted weapon or lure to build or deliver, because the vulnerability being exploited isn't a piece of code, it's an operational failure — a device that should never have been reachable from the public internet in the first place.

This matters for interpreting the campaign's actual intent. If the goal were narrowly psychological — "we can touch American water" as a single dramatic headline — a single high-profile incident, well-publicized, would achieve it more efficiently than a slow-building campaign that has grown from one state to at least twelve over roughly two weeks. The pattern instead looks like target-bank development at scale: Forescout's finding of 4,407 exposed Rockwell controllers alone (most of them not yet reduced by remediation) suggests the campaign's operators are working through a target list orders of magnitude larger than what's been publicly reported as "hit" so far. The psychological effect and the capability-building may not be competing explanations — they're plausibly the same action serving both purposes at once: each new incident both generates a headline and adds a mapped, characterized target to a bank that could be used for something more serious later, whether by the same actor or a successor campaign.

The TTP continuity from 2023 to 2026 — the same actor-alias cluster, the same "no new CVE needed" approach, the same pattern of causing disruption and fear without crossing into confirmed physical harm — isn't incidental. It's evidence of a stable, repeatable capability that keeps getting reused because the underlying condition that enables it (thousands of small municipal utilities adding remote/cellular connectivity to decades-old OT equipment faster than they can secure it) hasn't changed and isn't likely to change quickly. That same structural fact is also the reason attribution is genuinely hard here, independent of any political motivation to dispute it: the technique itself (find a PLC with a default password, log in, change something) requires no special access, no custom tooling, and no nation-state-level resourcing — it is trivially reproducible by a criminal crew, a hacktivist, or an actor deliberately imitating a well-documented prior campaign. The attribution signal rests almost entirely on TTP resemblance to a *publicly documented* 2023 case, which is exactly the kind of evidence that is easiest to fake for anyone motivated to look like Iran.

## Popper — How could we be wrong?

1. **Euclid's "mostly psychological, capability-building" framing understates what CISA actually documented.** The advisory states plainly that at one US victim, safety shutdown and alarm logic was specifically disabled — not incidental collateral damage from a credential change, but a deliberate modification that removes a safety mechanism. That is a genuine escalation in operational risk, not just symbolic disruption, and it's the kind of change that could cause real harm in a scenario only slightly different from the ones observed so far — for instance, if it coincided with an actual equipment fault the disabled alarm would otherwise have caught.
2. **"No formal attribution" is being read as equivalent to "genuinely unknown," and that's not how attribution normally works.** Formal US attribution of nation-state campaigns often lags technical confidence by months — SolarWinds is a comparable case where high-confidence private assessments preceded formal government attribution by a substantial margin. Treating the current "preliminary, likely Iran" state as meaningfully uncertain, rather than as the normal early phase of a process that usually firms up, may be overcautious.
3. **Trump's public rejection is being treated as informationally neutral in this report, but it's a political statement, not counter-evidence.** No technical basis for "Minnesota did this to itself" has been made public by the administration, and Governor Walz's response — that Trump "knows exactly who is responsible" — suggests the dismissal may reflect motivated reasoning (avoiding acknowledgment of an unanswered attack during an active conflict posture with Iran) rather than a genuine alternative assessment. This report risks false balance by giving the political dismissal equal narrative weight to the intelligence community's technical assessment.
4. **The false-flag caveat is doing more work in this report's framing than the sourcing supports.** Every mention of it found in this research is a process caveat — investigators "also probing" the possibility — not a competing hypothesis with its own evidence. No source proposes a specific alternative actor or offers technical grounds to doubt the Iran assessment beyond the general observation that the technique is easy to imitate.

## Seldon — What is likely to happen next?

Resolving each of Popper's points in turn, not just acknowledging them: **(1)** is accepted — Euclid's framing is revised. The campaign is better described as opportunistic, scale-based capability development *with a demonstrated ceiling of genuine operational-safety impact*, not purely psychological signaling. The absence of confirmed drinking-water harm to date most likely reflects some combination of target selection (smaller systems, fewer critical fail-safes removed so far), possible actor restraint (crossing into unambiguous physical harm would likely trigger a much sharper US response), and simple opportunity limits — not proof that future risk is low. **(2)** is accepted with a caveat kept separate: the epistemic question (is it probably Iran?) and the political question (will the US government act on that?) are different questions, and this report should not let the second quietly answer the first. Treating "preliminary, consistent tradecraft, no formal attribution yet" as *more likely than not* accurate, consistent with how comparable campaigns have resolved historically, is the right calibration — while still reporting plainly that the administration's public posture currently blocks any coordinated deterrent response, regardless of the technical assessment's accuracy. **(3)** is accepted — this report should not present Trump's rejection as evidentially neutral; it is a political claim unsupported by public technical evidence, and Walz's characterization is at least as well-supported by the available reporting. **(4)** is accepted — the false-flag possibility is downgraded from a competing hypothesis to a process caveat worth one sentence, not equal narrative weight.

**Three forecasts, each a range with a median — not a point estimate:**

- **Time until a formal, named US government attribution to Iran (an on-record agency statement, not anonymous sourcing):** the range runs from **never**, given the current administration's demonstrated public reluctance to attribute this specific incident to Iran, to about **18 months**, with a median around **6 to 9 months**. The short end is driven by mounting pressure from affected-state officials and by technical staff at CISA/EPA who reportedly already hold higher confidence than the public statements reflect; the long end reflects the real possibility that the political incentive to avoid the Iran narrative persists as long as the current administration does.
- **Additional US states or utilities reporting similar incidents over the next 3 months:** given the scale of exposure Forescout already documented (4,407 exposed Rockwell controllers alone, most not yet remediated), the range runs from a handful of additional isolated incidents to another mass-coordinated event comparable in scale to the Minnesota incident, with a median of a **steady, roughly monthly drip of isolated single-utility incidents** rather than another mass event — a patient actor benefiting from sustained low-grade disruption has less reason to repeat a high-visibility mass action that draws disproportionate federal attention.
- **Time until a confirmed instance of actual water-safety or contamination harm from this specific technique, if the campaign continues at its current pace and target profile:** this is the report's single highest-stakes open question, and the honest range is wide — from **under a year**, if any future target lacks adequate physical or procedural backup safeguards behind its compromised PLC, to **several years or never**, if the campaign continues favoring smaller systems and the actor continues avoiding unambiguous physical harm. There isn't a defensible median to collapse this to; both ends are reasoned judgment, not measured data, and the CISA-documented safety-logic-disabling incident is the clearest evidence that the capability for the short end already exists.
