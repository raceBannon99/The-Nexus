# 2026-09-04: What Is the Probability Wyden's VPN-Phaseout Letter Has Any Impact?

**Question:** On July 27, 2026, Senator Wyden sent a letter to Gregory Barbaccia (Federal CIO, OMB), Nick Andersen (Acting Director, CISA), and Dr. Arvind Raman (Under Secretary of Commerce for Standards and Technology, NIST) demanding the elimination of VPNs from federal networks in favor of Zero-Trust Architecture, across five specific asks: (1) CISA sets a two-year deadline for civilian agencies to replace VPN/remote-access technology; (2) NSA sets a similar deadline for military/intelligence/national-security networks; (3) NIST publishes standards mandating outbound-only remote-access architecture, memory-safe programming languages, decentralized/organization-held encryption keys, and Post-Quantum Cryptography alignment; (4) OMB directs civilian agencies to prioritize zero-trust investment and VPN decommissioning in budget cycles; (5) CISA/NSA/NIST/OMB coordinate a FAR/DFARS update banning VPN procurement absent vendor attestation of NIST zero-trust compliance. What is the probability this letter will have any impact — broken down by precedent from past Wyden oversight letters, independent threat/adversary urgency, and per-demand forecast bands at 12-month and 2–3-year horizons?

## Synthesis (Agent Bradlee)

The single fact that reframes this whole question: **CISA already issued BOD 26-02 on February 5, 2026** — five and a half months before Wyden's letter — ordering civilian agencies to inventory and, by August 2027, fully remove end-of-support edge devices (routers, firewalls, VPN gateways) exploited by nation-state actors. The exact underlying problem Wyden is writing about was already being acted on through an existing legal mechanism, for reasons that have nothing to do with this letter. That single fact should recalibrate "will this letter have impact" away from "will VPNs get phased out" (partially already happening, already ordered) and toward the narrower, more honest question: does *this letter specifically* change the speed, scope, or shape of what was already in motion?

Split by the letter's five asks, the picture is uneven, not uniform. The CISA and OMB asks (deadline-setting, budget prioritization) sit closest to existing authority and an existing directive already moving in the same direction — the range runs from about 35% to 65%, median near 50%, that some visible CISA/OMB action citing this general problem area happens within 12 months, though a clean "two-year full VPN sunset BOD" as literally specified is less likely (15–35%, median ~25%) than an extension or amendment folding VPNs explicitly into BOD 26-02's existing scope. The NIST ask has real precedent for narrow technical guidance emerging from a Wyden letter (his 2019 zip-file letter produced an NIST ITL Bulletin in 13 months and a full SP revision in 24), but this ask is far more sweeping — mandating specific programming-language safety properties and key-custody architecture is a different order of technical and political complexity, so 12-month odds of full standards are low (10–25%, median ~15%) while 2–3-year odds of *some* version are moderate (35–55%, median ~45%). The NSA ask is plausible specifically because NSPM-12 (signed six weeks before the letter) newly grants the NSA Director binding-directive authority it didn't clearly have before — a real, dated, verifiable enabling condition, putting 2–3-year odds at roughly 30–50% (median ~40%). The FAR/DFARS ask is the outlier: this requires formal interagency notice-and-comment rulemaking across two regulatory bodies, and the closest comparable rulemakings (the FAR CUI rule: 14 years from EO to rule; the EO 14028 cybersecurity FAR rules: proposed 2023, still not finalized in September 2026) put realistic odds of a finalized rule inside 2–3 years at 5–15% (median ~8%) — a letter has essentially no causal leverage over a process this institutionally slow.

Popper's two objections both survive into the forecast rather than getting argued away: a letter's incremental effect is genuinely hard to isolate from a trend already in motion (BOD 26-02, NSPM-12, and OMB's own M-22-09/M-25-04 zero-trust push all predate or run parallel to it), and a Republican-controlled executive branch has some incentive to *not* visibly act on a minority senator's demand regardless of merit, which the record can't rule out. Net answer: high probability (~75–90%) the letter registers as one more input into policy already trending this direction; low probability (~10–20%) any of its five specific asks gets enacted as literally written within 2–3 years, and the FAR/DFARS ask specifically is closer to zero.

## What Are the Facts? (Agent Sherlock)

**The load-bearing new fact: CISA BOD 26-02 predates the letter.** Issued February 5, 2026 — 172 days before Wyden's letter — [BOD 26-02](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/directives/bod-26-02-mitigating-risk-end-support-edge-devices) orders civilian agencies to inventory end-of-support (EOS) edge devices (routers, firewalls, load balancers, VPN gateways) by May 5, 2026, begin removal by February 5, 2027, and complete removal by August 5, 2027 — an 18-month sunset timeline for a *subset* of what Wyden's letter targets (EOS devices specifically, not all legacy internet-facing VPNs regardless of support status). A related [BOD on prioritizing security updates based on risk](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/directives/bod-26-04-implementation-guidance-prioritizing-security-updates-based-risk) (BOD 26-04) followed in June 2026, also before the letter. **This means CISA was already moving on the underlying problem, via existing legal authority, for reasons unconnected to this letter, before it was sent.**

**Precedent — what happened after Wyden's past letters of this general type:**

**Reading the pattern:** Wyden's letters produce real agency output when the ask is (a) narrow, (b) technical rather than regulatory, and (c) within the agency's existing publication authority — the NIST zip-file case is the clean positive precedent, but it took over a year even for a low-stakes, uncontroversial ask. When the ask requires new legislation or a formal regulatory ban (IoT Act, the two 2024 bills), outcomes split sharply: IoT succeeded but took ~3 years and required full bipartisan bill passage, not letter pressure alone; both 2024 procurement/standards bills have stalled with no floor action after well over a year. **No precedent case in this set shows a letter alone producing a binding regulatory mandate (a BOD or a FAR rule) — every binding outcome required either a formal directive process already underway for independent reasons (BOD 26-02) or full legislation.**

**FAR/DFARS rulemaking timeline, independent of Wyden:** the [FAR CUI rule](https://summit7.us/blog/far-cui-rule-major-hurdle) took over 14 years from its enabling Executive Order (13556, 2010) to a finalized rule. The FAR Council's own cybersecurity rules implementing [Executive Order 14028](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/10/03/2023-21327/federal-acquisition-regulation-standardizing-cybersecurity-requirements-for-unclassified-federal) were proposed October 3, 2023, and — per the most recent regulatory-agenda tracking found — remain unfinalized as of mid-2026, nearly three years later. **This is the single strongest piece of evidence that the FAR/DFARS ask specifically sits outside any realistic near-or-medium-term timeline, regardless of Wyden's or any other letter's pressure.**

**Independent threat-urgency evidence:** [VulnCheck's 2026 network-edge report](https://www.vulncheck.com/blog/network-edge-device-report-2026) documents 181 known-exploited edge-device vulnerabilities from 2025 alone, of which only 23.7% ever appeared in CISA's own KEV catalog — a significant blind-spot finding independent of this letter — and 42.5% affected end-of-life or near-end-of-life devices, the exact device class BOD 26-02 targets. This is real, independently-documented urgency: the pressure to retire legacy edge devices is backed by measurable exploitation data, not manufactured by the letter.

**No formal agency response to the July 27 letter has been publicly reported as of this report's date** (Sept. 4, 2026) — the most recent coverage found confirms none of the three agencies had issued a public reply or announced letter-specific action.

## What Must Be Fundamentally True? (Agent Euclid)

A letter's causal power is bounded by what it can actually change: it cannot grant new legal authority, cannot appropriate money, and cannot compel an executive-branch agency controlled by the opposing party. What it *can* do is (a) create a public, citable record that raises the political cost of inaction, (b) supply agencies already inclined to act with external cover or urgency to move faster, and (c) occasionally hand an agency a fully-worked technical argument (like the CRS memo attached to this letter) that saves staff the work of building one internally. Given that, the right frame for "will this letter have impact" isn't "will VPNs get phased out" — BOD 26-02 already answers a meaningful chunk of that independent of Wyden — but "does this letter change the *marginal* trajectory of something already moving."

Splitting the five asks by what mechanism would have to produce each one clarifies the forecast:

- **CISA (two-year civilian deadline) and OMB (budget-cycle prioritization):** both sit inside existing agency authority, and BOD 26-02/26-04 show CISA is already using that authority on the same underlying device class. The most likely path isn't a brand-new directive citing Wyden's letter — it's a future BOD amendment or OMB budget-cycle guidance that happens to satisfy the letter's ask as a byproduct of a process already running. Attribution to the letter, if it happens, will be genuinely hard to isolate.
- **NIST (technical standards):** the zip-file precedent shows NIST *will* publish in response to a Wyden letter when the ask fits its normal publication process — but that took over a year for something far narrower than "mandate memory-safe languages and decentralized key custody across the federal ZTNA stack." This ask is also substantially already in motion via OMB M-22-09/M-25-04's existing zero-trust pillars, independent of Wyden.
- **NSA:** the only ask where a *new* legal mechanism (NSPM-12) opened right before the letter, giving this specific ask a plausible causal story the others lack — but "plausible mechanism exists" is different from "will be used inside 2-3 years."
- **FAR/DFARS:** categorically different in kind, not degree. This requires two statutory bodies to run notice-and-comment rulemaking jointly, and the closest comparable rulemakings (FAR CUI: 14 years; EO 14028 FAR rules: still pending after 3 years) show this process operates on a timescale where no single letter, from any senator of any party, is a material variable.

The unifying principle: letters influence *how agencies use authority they already have or are actively acquiring*; they do not create authority, money, or bipartisan legislative consensus from nothing. Every historical case where a Wyden ask became binding (IoT Act, and arguably BOD 26-02's alignment with this letter) either ran through full legislation or an already-independently-moving executive process — never a letter as the sole mechanism.

Library candidate flagged: the three-tier mechanism split above (agency-discretionary action / agency publication within existing authority / formal rulemaking or legislation) is a reusable lens for scoring *any* congressional letter's five-or-however-many asks by realistic causal pathway, rather than treating all asks in a single letter as equally likely.

## How Could We Be Wrong? (Agent Popper)

Two real objections:

**Objection 1 — the report may be crediting BOD 26-02 as evidence the letter is redundant/irrelevant, when it could equally be read as evidence the letter is well-timed rather than unnecessary.** Euclid's framing risks implying "CISA was already doing this, so the letter doesn't matter" — but an oversight letter landing five months into an already-running 18-month directive could plausibly *tighten* that directive's scope, pace, or enforcement rather than being redundant with it. The report hasn't actually checked whether BOD 26-02's scope (EOS devices specifically) is narrower than what Wyden is asking (all legacy internet-facing VPNs, EOS or not) — if there's a real gap between the two, the letter has a specific, checkable thing left to accomplish rather than nothing.

**Objection 2 — the precedent table leans heavily on a small, cherry-pickable sample, and mixes genuinely comparable and non-comparable cases.** Five data points, of which only one (the zip-file letter) is a clean "letter alone, no legislation" case with a positive outcome — and it's a much lower-stakes, lower-cost ask than mandating memory-safe rewrites or decentralized key custody across the federal VPN estate. Generalizing from "NIST published a bulletin about zip files" to "NIST will publish sweeping ZTNA standards" glosses over a real difference in scope and cost, and the report should say so rather than treating the precedent as equally load-bearing across all five asks.

## What Is Likely to Happen Next? (Agent Seldon)

Resolving each objection rather than logging it:

**On Objection 1 (BOD 26-02 redundant vs. well-timed):** Popper is right that "already moving" doesn't mean "already sufficient," and the report is revised to state the actual gap plainly: BOD 26-02 targets *end-of-support* edge devices on an 18-month sunset; Wyden's ask targets *all* legacy internet-facing VPN/remote-access systems regardless of support status, on a two-year sunset. These overlap heavily (most legacy VPN gateways cited in the letter's own examples — Ivanti, Check Point, older Fortinet/Cisco gear — are EOS or approaching it) but aren't identical in scope, meaning there is a real, non-trivial gap the letter is pushing on: currently-supported-but-architecturally-legacy VPN products that BOD 26-02's EOS trigger wouldn't reach on its own. This raises, not lowers, the probability that some visible CISA action *broadens* BOD 26-02's scope or timeline rather than merely reiterating it — reflected in the CISA/OMB forecast band below.

**On Objection 2 (precedent sample too small/uneven):** Popper is right, and the forecast bands below are widened and explicitly split by ask-cost/complexity rather than applying one precedent uniformly. The zip-file case is treated as informative only for "will NIST publish something in this direction eventually," not for timeline or completeness — a higher-cost, higher-complexity ask like this one is expected to take longer and land less completely than that precedent alone would suggest, which is why the NIST band below carries a wider, later-median range than a naive read of the zip-file case would produce.

**Forward-looking forecasts** (ranges with medians, not point probabilities, per standing convention — these refine and, where evidence conflicts, supersede the prior report's single BOD-focused forecast):

- *CISA/OMB: some visible action within 12 months that plausibly satisfies part of the letter's ask (a BOD 26-02 scope amendment, new BOD, or OMB budget-cycle guidance explicitly naming legacy VPN/remote-access sunset), whether or not credited to the letter:* the range runs from about **40% to 65%, with a median around 55%** — high because CISA is already structurally active in this exact space and OMB's M-22-09/M-25-04 cadence already produces recurring zero-trust guidance on a roughly annual cycle. *A clean, full two-year civilian-wide VPN-specific BOD matching the letter's literal ask, within 12 months:* narrower, **15% to 30%, median ~20%**.
- *NIST: publication of some form of ZTNA/remote-access implementation guidance citing or plausibly responsive to this letter, within 2–3 years:* **30% to 50%, median ~40%** — grounded in the zip-file precedent's positive outcome but widened later and lower than that precedent alone suggests, given this ask's far greater technical scope (memory-safe language mandates, decentralized key custody, PQC alignment are each substantial standards efforts in their own right, more plausibly delivered as separate publications over years than one unified standard).
- *NSA: a parallel binding directive for national-security systems, within 2–3 years:* **30% to 50%, median ~40%** — supported by NSPM-12's real, dated grant of authority, tempered by the fact that new authorities typically take multiple budget/planning cycles to convert into an issued directive.
- *FAR/DFARS: a finalized rule barring non-compliant VPN procurement, within 2–3 years:* **3% to 12%, median ~6%** — the FAR CUI rule (14 years) and the still-unfinalized EO 14028 FAR rules (3+ years and counting) are the closest comparables, and neither involved anything as institutionally slow-moving finishing faster because of senatorial pressure. *Within 5 years:* wider, **10% to 30%, median ~18%**.
- *Any measurable, attributable acceleration in the pre-existing zero-trust/legacy-VPN-retirement trend specifically because of this letter (as opposed to BOD 26-02, NSPM-12, and OMB's own cadence, which would produce action regardless):* this is the hardest quantity to estimate and the most honest way to answer "will the letter itself have impact" — **10% to 30%, median ~18%**, reflecting that isolating one letter's marginal contribution from three independent, already-moving forcing functions is inherently difficult, and the honest expectation is that most of whatever happens next would have happened on a similar timeline without it.
