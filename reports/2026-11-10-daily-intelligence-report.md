**France formally blames Iran's IRGC for the October refrigeration sabotage, which it says caused at least 15 fires.** A five-page report published Monday by France's Cyber Crisis Coordination Centre (which brings together ANSSI, the military's cyber command, and the DGSE and DGSI intelligence services) attributes the attack on refrigeration controllers at about 700 French grocery sites to Shahid Kaveh. That unit sits under the IRGC's Electronic and Cyber Center, and US agencies identify it as CyberAv3ngers, the group behind the 2023 attacks on US water utilities. The report names and pictures the unit's commanders and says the same hackers hit Schneider Electric equipment at several small hydroelectric plants, with moderate disruption. Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot called the sabotage unprecedented in scale and aggressiveness. France summoned Iran's ambassador and will push for EU sanctions, and Paris prosecutors opened a judicial investigation and are seeking international arrest warrants. The report doesn't address the November second wave across at least 11 European countries, which remains unattributed. Ryan moved this campaign from the Unclear table to Iran and raised it from Tier 3 to Tier 1. [The Record](https://therecord.media/france-blames-iran-for-fridge-cyberattacks); [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/france-blames-iran-for-sabotage-attack.html)

**Correction: BOSSAI is one man in Taiwan, not a suspected Chinese actor.** Acronis's full analysis says with high confidence that a single individual in Hualien City, Taiwan, ran the BOSSAI AI-agent operation. He reused the same identity across his legitimate businesses and his attack servers, and his home IP address appeared in one of his Grok sessions. His 12 agents (9 in use) aimed at hundreds of organizations in at least 11 countries. They pulled 119 emergency-dispatch records for seven Taipei districts, found a fire-department platform exposing patient ECG data and ambulance video from 2018, broke into a Taiwanese lender through SQL injection, took data from three Thai universities, and collected about 206,800 Mexican home-phone records. Grok and MiniMax refused direct attack requests and asked for proof of authorization, so the attack agents ran on "abliterated" DeepSeek, GLM, and Qwen models with their safety training removed. The agents also invented CVEs and reported attacks that never happened. Acronis thinks he probably wanted money and says it's unclear whether he works for anyone. On 11-06 the Adversary Tracking Report filed BOSSAI under China, based on a newsletter summary rather than Acronis's own report. That filing was wrong. Ryan moved it to Cybercrime at Tier 2. [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/taiwan-based-hacker-used-bossai-ai.html)

## Zero Trust Tactics

**CISA adds four actively exploited flaws to the KEV catalog, including SSHole.** The four: an OpenBSD OpenSSH authentication bypass on Linux (CVE-2026-123158, "SSHole," CVSS 8.1), an IBM Aspera Faspex authorization flaw (CVE-2026-123218), a SignalWire FreeSWITCH code-injection flaw (CVE-2026-116779), and an unauthenticated-admin flaw in the GPUStack AI-model server (CVE-2026-102895). GreyNoise saw its first SSHole exploitation attempt at 12:46 a.m. UTC on Nov. 7, under 24 hours after disclosure, and has since confirmed post-exploitation from six source IPs: reading password hashes and root SSH keys, querying cloud metadata, and planting authorized_keys entries for persistence. Crypto-miners and IoT botnets are among the attackers. [CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2026/11/09/cisa-adds-four-known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog); [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/cisa-warns-of-active-exploitation-of.html)

**SAP fixes four maximum-severity flaws that need no login.** November's Security Patch Day fixes four CVSS 10.0 flaws. Three are in NetWeaver: in the Internet Communication Manager (code execution), the SAML 2.0 service provider (log in as any user on some SSO setups), and SAP Gateway (impersonate one of the system's own servers). The fourth, in the internet-facing Web Dispatcher, also allows code execution. None requires user interaction. [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/sap-patches-four-cvss-100-flaws.html)

**Chrome patches its eighth exploited zero-day of 2026.** CVE-2026-125350 is a use-after-free in Chrome's XML component, reported Nov. 6 by an anonymous researcher. Google says an exploit exists in the wild and hasn't said who is using it. [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/google-issues-chrome-security-update.html)

**N-able pulls hosted N-central offline after 33 zero-days go public.** Researcher Sean Bales published more than 30 N-central flaws, collectively called Able2Shell, with proof-of-concept exploits. They chain into anonymous credential theft, administrator creation, and root code execution. N-able took its hosted environments offline in all regions and told on-premises customers to shut their servers down until a hotfix ships. It says it has seen no exploitation so far. N-central is a remote-management tool used by MSPs, so a compromise reaches every client it manages. [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/n-able-takes-hosted-n-central-offline.html)

**A default "admin/admin" password let researchers unlock and start two automakers' cars.** Shubham Shah and Sam Curry found that both brands' apps signed customers in through ForgeRock (now Ping Identity), and that each OpenIDM server still had its built-in Apache Felix admin console, using the documented default username and password. ForgeRock's own hardening guide says to remove that console before going live. The console was blocked from the internet, but the edge and the Java backend parsed URLs differently, so encoded slashes got through. That gave full account takeover and, on connected cars, the ability to unlock doors and start engines. The researchers tested only their own and consenting owners' cars in February. Both companies fixed it within about two hours. [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/default-adminadmin-server-password.html)

**One researcher with consumer AI models found 100+ serious flaws in UK government code.** ControlPlane's Chris Nesbitt-Smith scanned 349 public-sector codebases from 33 organizations using Claude's Opus and Sonnet models on a laptop, and found vulnerabilities in about two-thirds of them. The worst: the Planning Inspectorate had committed Microsoft Entra ID credentials to GitHub and never fully removed them. They didn't expire and gave "god-tier" read and write access to every SharePoint site and employee OneDrive, including HR records and evidence under legal hold. The agency says its monitoring caught his activity, it fixed the problem within 20 minutes, and no data was taken. He also found flaws in Department for Education services and a Scottish Government data portal. All have been fixed. His point: "It's all daft stuff" a routine health check would catch, and closing public repositories without fixing them is the worst option, since adversaries have probably already copied them. [WIRED](https://www.wired.com/story/security-flaws-across-uk-government-sites-exposed-god-tier-information/)

**Resilient Cyber: "We can't patch our way out."** Chris Hughes, building on Tal Be'ery's "Dense in Depth," argues that AI-driven discovery has shown software bugs to be dense rather than sparse, so no amount of patching will exhaust them. His evidence: 88,886 CVEs published by Oct. 29, against FIRST's June projection of about 66,000 for all of 2026, and GPT-6 Cyber finding nearly 1,000 Linux privilege-escalation bugs in a codebase reviewed for decades. Exploitation still needs its own denominator: Empirical Security's Michael Roytman counts 696 exploited out of 83,167 CVEs published Jan. 1–Oct. 19. Hughes's conclusion is to keep shortening patch cycles but invest more in limiting what an exploit can achieve once it lands. [Resilient Cyber](https://www.resilientcyber.io/p/theres-no-bucket-big-enough). **Newsletter Tracker:** this *Supports* Thread 3 ("Resilience, not prevention, is the affordable strategy for most organizations"; status: Holding, current statement ["Peacemaker for Resilience"](https://diffuser.substack.com/p/peacemaker-for-resilience), Aug 2025). If remediation can't drain the supply of bugs, surviving the intrusions that get through is the affordable strategy. It also bears on the open tension with Thread 4: cheap AI discovery helps attackers at least as much as defenders.

**EFF and the ACLU ask the Sixth Circuit to require warrants for license-plate-reader searches.** In *United States v. Sturdivant*, Cleveland-area police ran an open-ended search of two ALPR databases (Flock and ELSAG) to find a robbery suspect's car. The district court held he had no privacy interest in a plate anyone can see. The amicus brief argues ALPR networks are "a virtual time machine" for anyone's past movements, so searching them is a Fourth Amendment search. Last month a federal district court called ALPRs "a form of indiscriminate mass surveillance." [EFF Deeplinks](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/11/were-asking-appeals-court-require-warrant-alpr-searches)

[Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/virginia-lawmakers-assess-misuse-of-automated-license-plate-readers/) (via FFXnow's Nov. 9 Morning Notes)

**Russia adds Apple and Microsoft to its surveillance registry.** Roskomnadzor's registry obliges listed companies to keep user activity logs for three years and hand data to authorities on request. ## Cyber Crime & Fraud

**Pending Evidence Queue item (submitted 11-07): a 30-study evidence base on cyber fraud and what reduces it.** Rick's local CSV (`Nexus Artifacts Library/cyber_fraud_evidence.csv`) codes 30 academic studies published 2006–2025, searched 2026-11-07. It's a single-coder provisional extraction, so read it as a starting map, not a meta-analysis. And it's research, not this year's incidents. The patterns:
- **Phishing training has mixed evidence.** Educational materials reduced simulated information entry by 40% in a 1,001-person roleplay study, but some materials also reduced clicks on legitimate links ([Sheng et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1145/1753326.1753383)). Embedded training reduced susceptibility at one 924-person financial institution ([2018](https://openalex.org/W3088348648)). A 2025 set of three randomized field experiments challenges the assumed efficacy of embedded training and points to delayed, non-embedded feedback instead ([MISQ, 2025](https://doi.org/10.25300/misq/2025/19354)). Message framing had no significant effect in a workplace spear-phishing experiment ([2013](https://doi.org/10.1109/msp.2013.106)).
- **Warnings and toolbars underperformed.** All three security toolbars tested in 2006 failed to prevent phishing ([Wu et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/1124772.1124863)). Visual deception beat standard browser cues for many users ([Dhamija et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/1124772.1124861)).
- **Detection works in benchmarks, not yet in loss data.** One classifier caught over 96% of phishing with ~0.1% false positives ([2007](https://doi.org/10.1145/1242572.1242660)). Twitter disabled 95% of several million fraudulent accounts using marketplace intelligence ([2013](https://openalex.org/W1815362064)). None of these studies measures realized financial losses.
- **The crime economy.** Botnet services are widely available at low advertised prices across 36,314 dark-web listings ([2023](https://doi.org/10.1109/ecrime61234.2023.10485500)). A few corporate-like ransomware groups dominate ([NBER, 2023](https://doi.org/10.3386/w30834)). Direct ransomware payments were at least $12.8M in Bitcoin over 2013–mid-2017 ([2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/cybsec/tyz003)). The World Cybercrime Index finds offender geography concentrated and specialized, with Russia a recurring hub ([PLOS ONE, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0297312)).

The category matched its content; no mismatch to flag. *(Hotlinks go to each study's DOI or OpenAlex record. The CSV itself is a local vault file with no URL.)*

## Over the Horizon Technology Trends

**DeepMind's AI agents make legacy RSA-1024 cheaper to attack.** Working with cryptographers from UC San Diego and Inria, DeepMind found two shortcuts. (1) For PKCS#1 v1.5 signatures, the format behind the signing interface of many hardware security modules, padding adds "no protection at all" once an attacker controls about half the signed block. Two agents, with about 20 mostly approving messages from a human, re-ran an academic forgery attack in under four days and cut CPU time 3.5-fold (391 core-years versus 1,380). The attack forges a signature without recovering the key, and it needs about 4 billion signing queries in advance. (2) A batch-factoring method makes each additional 1024-bit key about 30 times cheaper to factor, though only on paper so far. Modern 2048-bit keys aren't affected. DeepMind's Pushmeet Kohli: "We cannot treat cryptographic hardness as a fixed assumption." [SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/google-deepminds-ai-agents-make-1024-bit-rsa-keys-cheaper-to-attack/)

**Google DeepMind's AI formally verifies the Signal protocol.** DeepMind's AlphaProof Code framework formally proved a number of Signal's key cryptographic guarantees in four weeks, and correctly failed to verify older versions with known flaws. It covered the protocol, not the whole app. DeepMind's stretch goal is to do the same for "all the software of the world," but cost and scale are open questions. [WIRED](https://www.wired.com/story/a-new-ai-tool-from-google-deepmind-formally-verified-the-signal-protocol/) *Also covered above under AGI Arrival Timeline.*
