# Broken Windows, Stop-and-Frisk, and the NYC Crime Drop: Fact-Checking a Gladwell Book Review

**Date:** 2026-11-07
**Question:** Rick is reviewing Malcolm Gladwell's *The Tipping Point* (2000) and *Revenge of the Tipping Point* (2024). His draft says NYC added stop-and-frisk to Broken Windows in the late 1990s, a federal judge "tossed out" stop-and-frisk in 2013, everyone expected crime to skyrocket, and instead crime "exponentially went down compared to other cities." (1) Is that correct? (2) Did the judge throw out all of Broken Windows or just stop-and-frisk? (3) Rick's thesis: ending stop-and-frisk didn't matter, but maybe enforcing low-level crime laws did. Does the evidence support that?

---

## The Answer (Bradlee)

Your passage is mostly right but needs three fixes. Your thesis is half right. The evidence supports the first half: ending stop-and-frisk did not bring crime back. It cuts against the second half. New York cut its low-level enforcement at the same time, roughly halving misdemeanor arrests between 2010 and 2019, and crime kept falling anyway.

Start with the ruling. On August 12, 2013, U.S. District Judge Shira Scheindlin ruled in *Floyd v. City of New York* that the NYPD's stop-and-frisk *practice* violated the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments. Too many stops lacked reasonable suspicion, and too many targeted Black and Latino men. She did not ban stop-and-frisk. The Supreme Court upheld the basic tactic in *Terry v. Ohio* (1968), and she said explicitly that she was not ordering an end to it. She ordered reforms and appointed a federal monitor. Broken Windows enforcement (summonses and misdemeanor arrests for turnstile-jumping, public drinking, graffiti) was never before the court. The appeals court froze her order and removed her from the case that October. Mayor Bill de Blasio dropped the city's appeal in early 2014 and accepted the reforms.

Next, the timeline. Stops were not a late-1990s add-on that went straight to scale. The aggressive Street Crime Unit and a 1999 state attorney general investigation do date from the late 1990s. But mass stop-and-frisk was a Bloomberg-era phenomenon. Stops rose about 600 percent and peaked at 685,724 in 2011. Most of the city's historic crime drop, from 2,245 murders in 1990, had already happened before that. And stops were already falling before the judge ruled: down to 191,558 in 2013, then to about 12,000 by 2016.

Then the outcome. Crime did not skyrocket. Murders went from 333 in 2014 to about 290 in 2017, the fewest since the early 1950s. In 2025 the city recorded its fewest shootings on record and roughly 305 murders. "Exponentially," though, overstates it. The decline was steady, not explosive, and murders even rose about 6 percent in 2015. The honest comparison: national murders jumped 10.8 percent in 2015 while New York's rose far less and then resumed falling. New York outperformed the country without stop-and-frisk.

Now the thesis. If low-level enforcement were the active ingredient, cutting it should have hurt. New York cut it hard. Misdemeanor arrests fell by about half from their 2010 peak. Quality-of-life summonses dropped sharply. Marijuana and fare-evasion enforcement were scaled back. The city's own Inspector General for the NYPD studied 1.8 million summonses and 650,000 misdemeanor arrests from 2010 to 2015. It found "no evidence" that this enforcement drove felony crime down, and no rise in felonies as it fell. So the post-2013 period is the same natural experiment for both tactics, and it comes out the same way for both.

There is a narrower version of your thesis the evidence does support. The 1990s research is genuinely mixed. One NBER study found a modest effect of misdemeanor arrests on robbery and car theft. Harcourt and Ludwig's reanalysis found no support for the core Broken Windows claim. The largest review, a 2024 meta-analysis of 59 tests, finds that "disorder policing" does reduce crime. But the effect comes from fixing disorderly places, through problem-solving and community work at hot spots. Aggressive arrest-driven order maintenance produced no significant reduction. That is closer to Wilson and Kelling's original idea, literally fixing the windows, than to what NYPD built on it.

Gladwell himself has conceded the larger point. In his 2024 TED talk he said "I was wrong" to present Broken Windows as the settled explanation. His revised view: crime falls through "the intelligent and thoughtful and selective application of police authority," not indiscriminate policing. He treats stop-and-frisk and misdemeanor enforcement as one package, though, so your instinct to separate them is a fair critique of both books. The evidence just doesn't rescue the arrest half of the package.

**Bottom line for the review:** keep "stop-and-frisk didn't matter." Change "low-level enforcement did" to "fixing disorder may help; arresting people for it hasn't been shown to."

## The Facts (Sherlock)

### 1. What the 2013 ruling actually did

- **Ruling:** *Floyd v. City of New York*, S.D.N.Y., Judge Shira Scheindlin, August 12, 2013. She found the City liable under the **Fourth Amendment** (stops without reasonable suspicion) and the **Fourteenth Amendment** (indirect racial profiling).
- **She did not ban stop-and-frisk.** Terry stops are constitutional under *Terry v. Ohio* (1968), and Scheindlin said explicitly she was not ordering a wholesale end to the practice. Her remedy was court-supervised reform: changes to stop procedures, plus a federal monitor.
- **Broken Windows was not at issue.** The case concerned street stops only. Quality-of-life summonses and misdemeanor arrests were outside its scope.
- **Appeal:** On October 31, 2013, the Second Circuit stayed her remedial order and removed her from the case, citing her media interviews. In January 2014, new Mayor de Blasio dropped the appeal as promised. The City then agreed to comply with the remedial order, with the monitor's term to be limited to three years.

### 2. The stop-and-frisk timeline

- **1990s:** Street stops are a long-standing police tool. The NYPD's **Street Crime Unit** was its most aggressive stop unit, filing 27,000+ stop reports in 1998. Four of its officers killed Amadou Diallo in February 1999. That prompted Attorney General Eliot Spitzer's 1999 study of ~175,000 stop reports, which found racial disparities and found that nearly one in four Street Crime Unit stop reports lacked a sufficient legal basis.
- **2002–2011:** Under Bloomberg/Kelly, stops rose roughly 600% and peaked at **685,724 in 2011**.
- **Before the ruling:** Stops were already falling. In 2013 they were down to **191,558**.
- **After:** Roughly **12,000 in 2016** and ~10,000–11,600 a year after that. That is a ~98% decline from the 2011 peak. NYCLU notes reported stops likely undercount actual stops.

So Rick's "late 1990s" is accurate for when aggressive stop-and-frisk *began drawing scrutiny*. But the mass program came in the 2000s, after most of the crime decline had already happened.

### 3. What crime did

| Year | NYC murders | Context |
|---|---|---|
| 1990 | 2,245 | Record high |
| 2011 | — | Stops peak at 685,724 |
| 2013 | — | *Floyd* ruling; stops fall to 191,558 |
| 2014 | 333 | De Blasio drops appeal |
| 2015 | 352 | +~6% in NYC; **national murders +10.8%**, biggest one-year jump since 1971 |
| 2016 | 335 | Stops ~12,000 |
| 2017 | ~290 (sources say 286–292) | Fewest since the early 1950s |
| 2025 | ~305 (preliminary) | Record-low 688 shooting incidents; murders down ~20% from 2024 |

"Crime didn't skyrocket" is **correct**. "Exponentially went down compared to other cities" is **not supported**: the decline was steady and modest, about 13% in murders from 2014 to 2017. The defensible comparison is that NYC held or kept falling while the national murder count spiked in 2015–2016. (The 2020–2021 pandemic-era spike, seen nationally, is outside the post-*Floyd* window and isn't analyzed here.)

### 4. What happened to low-level enforcement after 2013

- **Misdemeanor arrests** in NYC fell by about **half from their 2010 peak** by 2019 (Wall Street Journal, via John Jay College's Misdemeanor Justice Project data). John Jay's summary: enforcement could be de-prioritized "without an associated rise in crime rates."
- The **NYC Department of Investigation's Inspector General for the NYPD (June 2016)** studied 1,839,414 quality-of-life summonses, 650,000+ quality-of-life misdemeanor arrests, and 600,000+ felony complaints from 2010 to 2015. Finding: "no evidence to suggest that crime control can be directly attributed to issuing quality-of-life summonses and making misdemeanor arrests." There was no correlation over time, and summonses fell dramatically with no felony spike. The report's own caveats: it doesn't prove *zero* effect, and it made no finding on "the much broader concept of the 'Broken Windows' policing strategy." The NYPD under Bratton disputed it, and DOI stood by it.

### 5. The broader research on Broken Windows

- **Wilson & Kelling (1982), *The Atlantic*:** the original theory, that visible disorder signals no one is in charge and invites more serious crime.
- **Corman & Mocan (NBER, 2002–03):** using misdemeanor arrests as a proxy for Broken Windows in 1990s NYC, they found a real but limited effect, concentrated on **robbery and motor-vehicle theft**. A 10% rise in misdemeanor arrests went with a ~2.5–3.2% drop in robberies. The effect was smaller than advocates claimed.
- **Harcourt & Ludwig (2006), *U. Chicago Law Review*:** reanalyzed Kelling & Sousa's 2001 pro-Broken-Windows NYC study using 1989–1998 data, plus the Moving to Opportunity housing experiment. They found "no support for a simple first-order disorder-crime relationship." Their reanalysis attributes much of the precinct-level association to precincts reverting from crack-era highs.
- **Braga, Schnell & Welsh (2024), *Criminology & Public Policy*:** updated meta-analysis of 59 tests. Disorder policing overall produces **statistically significant crime reductions**, with spillover benefits to surrounding areas. The **strongest effects came from community and problem-solving interventions** that changed physical and social disorder at hot spots. **Aggressive order-maintenance strategies did not generate significant crime reductions.**

### 6. Gladwell's own reversal (*Revenge of the Tipping Point* and TED, October 2024)

- He says his 2000 chapter taught millions that the NYC crime drop "was all broken windows." He frames stop-and-frisk as "how the NYPD interpreted the broken windows theory."
- On *Floyd*: "everybody thought crime was going to go back up," including the judge. Instead NYC had "a second, even more miraculous decline," and "by 2019 New York City is as safe as Paris." He gives no figures for that.
- "I was wrong." His revised view: "It's not indiscriminate policing that causes crime to fall. Rather, it is the intelligent and thoughtful and selective application of police authority."
- **He does not separate** misdemeanor enforcement from stop-and-frisk. He treats them as one package ("stop and frisk and broken windows policing"). That is the gap Rick's thesis is probing.

## First-Principles Analysis (Euclid)

To credit a policy with a crime decline, three things must be true. The policy changed. Crime changed in the predicted direction *after* it. And nothing else that changed at the same time explains it equally well.

**Applied to stop-and-frisk:** the post-2013 case is about as clean a natural experiment as urban policing gets. A ~98% drop in stops was followed by continued crime decline. Whatever stops contributed at the margin, they were not load-bearing. The direction of the 1990s evidence agrees: the biggest crime decline came *before* stops scaled up.

**Applied to low-level enforcement:** the same test, run on the same years, gives the same answer. Misdemeanor arrests and summonses also fell steeply (by about half for arrests) during the period when crime fell. If enforcement were load-bearing, removing half of it should have shown up. It didn't, and the city's own watchdog found no correlation.

**What survives:** the Broken Windows *mechanism* (visible disorder invites crime) is not refuted by any of this. What's refuted is the claim that *arresting people for disorder offenses* is what makes the mechanism work. The 2024 meta-analysis draws exactly that line: fixing disorderly places works, and aggressive arrest-based enforcement does not. NYC in the 1990s did both. It cleaned graffiti off the subway system and scaled up misdemeanor arrests, and the two were never separated in the original story.

**First draft answer:** Rick's passage needs the judge's ruling and the "exponential" claim corrected. His thesis should be narrowed from "enforcing low-level laws worked" to "addressing disorder may work; arresting people for it hasn't been shown to."

**Library candidates:** concur with Sherlock.

## How Could We Be Wrong? (Popper)

1. **The 2010s are a weak test of 1990s policy.** By 2013, NYC was already low-crime, and a mature deterrence regime might persist after enforcement is scaled back (reputation, lagged effects). Enforcement may have mattered during the *descent* and become unnecessary at the bottom. So "it didn't matter after 2013" doesn't prove "it never mattered."
2. **Enforcement wasn't eliminated, only reduced.** Halving misdemeanor arrests still left a very large enforcement apparatus. The natural experiment tests the marginal effect of the extra half, not the whole policy.
3. **Confounders run the other way too.** Post-2013 NYC also used precision policing (gang takedowns, hot-spot focus), had a large police force, and benefited from technology. These could have *masked* harm from reduced enforcement. That is the same objection Rick's thesis raises against crediting stop-and-frisk's removal.
4. **The DOI report is narrow.** It covers 2010–2015 only, is correlational, and disclaims any finding on Broken Windows broadly.
5. **"Exponential" might be defensible loosely.** Rick may mean "dramatically," not mathematically. But in a published review, a reader can check it, and the data don't support a dramatic relative decline in 2014–2017.

---

## Resolution (Seldon)

No forecast question here, so there is no range. Popper's objections, resolved one by one:

1. **Stands, and it narrows the conclusion.** The report doesn't claim enforcement *never* mattered. It claims the evidence doesn't *show* it did, in either era: the 1990s literature is contested (Corman & Mocan modest; Harcourt & Ludwig null), and the 2010s natural experiment shows no harm from cutting it. Rick's thesis needs evidence *for* enforcement, and that is what's missing. The synthesis says "hasn't been shown," not "proven useless."
2. **Accepted, and the language reflects it.** The test is of halving enforcement, not eliminating it. But the same is true of stop-and-frisk, which was cut 98%, not zero. Rick's thesis treats one partial cut as decisive, so consistency requires treating the other the same way.
3. **Accepted as symmetric.** Precision policing confounds both tactics equally. It can't rescue enforcement without also rescuing stop-and-frisk. Gladwell's own revised explanation ("selective application of police authority") *is* precision policing, which is the most likely real driver of the post-2013 decline.
4. **Accepted, so the DOI report isn't relied on alone.** The conclusion rests on DOI plus the misdemeanor-arrest trend plus the 2024 meta-analysis, which covers many cities and decades.
5. **Resolved:** recommend replacing "exponentially" with a precise, checkable claim.

## Suggested Rewrite of the Review Passage

> The NYC Broken Windows experiment appeared to work. New York became one of the safest big cities in the world, and the pundits, helped along by Gladwell's book, credited Broken Windows. Over the next decade, the NYPD stretched the idea into mass "stop and frisk": by 2011, officers were stopping nearly 700,000 people a year, most of them young Black and Latino men who weren't breaking any law. They just looked like they might.
>
> In 2013, a federal judge ruled that the way the NYPD was doing it was unconstitutional (which it absolutely was) and ordered the department to reform. Stops fell by about 98 percent. Everyone, including the judge, expected crime to come roaring back. It didn't. In 2015, when murders jumped nearly 11 percent nationally, New York's rose about half as much, then resumed falling. Two years later, the city recorded its fewest murders since the early 1950s.
>
> But here's the part Gladwell skips. New York didn't just stop frisking people. Over the same decade, it cut misdemeanor arrests roughly in half, and crime kept falling anyway. The city's own police watchdog found no evidence that all those summonses and arrests for turnstile-jumping and public drinking had driven serious crime down. What the research does support is fixing the broken windows themselves: cleaning up disorderly places. Arresting the people standing near them is a different thing.
