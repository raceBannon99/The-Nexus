# Is Odysseus Really Chaotic Good? Checking the D&D Alignment Chart

**Question:** In the artifacts library, we have a D&D alignment chart that places Odysseus as Chaotic Good. I've just read the book and watched the Nolan movie. I'm not sure Odysseus is correctly placed.

## Bradlee — So, what's the answer?

Your instinct is right — Chaotic Good doesn't hold up, and there's an unusually clean second opinion available: Wizards of the Coast's own official D&D sourcebook, *Complete Scoundrel* (2007), places Odysseus at **Lawful Neutral**, in the same group as James Bond and Sanjuro from *Yojimbo*. That's not a fan theory — it's the actual publisher of D&D, in print, naming him specifically. The current artifact (`fact-sheets/dnd-alignment-chart-framework.png`) doesn't match it.

Here's why Lawful Neutral fits better than the chart's own axis definitions do for Chaotic Good. The chart itself defines Chaotic as "values freedom and impulse." That's close to the opposite of Odysseus's defining trait. His Homeric epithet, *polymetis* — "of many wiles" — and Emily Wilson's now-famous rendering of the poem's opening word, *polytropos*, as "complicated," both point at calculation, not impulse. He waits ten years at Troy, seven more getting home, refuses Calypso's offer of immortality to keep working the problem, ties himself to the mast instead of yielding to the Sirens, and — this is the part most relevant to "impulse" — spends the entire back half of the *Odyssey* in disguise, patiently gathering intelligence in his own hall before acting. Nolan's own framing of the character (per interviews around the 2026 film) is "an amazing strategist, and a very wily person," explicitly drawing on the Wilson translation's emphasis on cunning. None of that is what the chart's own "Chaotic" column describes.

The "Good" half needs a second look too. The chart's justification line — "breaks rules to save his people and return home" — doesn't actually match the text on its most important example. The single act of rule-breaking with the largest consequences in the whole poem is Odysseus taunting the blinded Polyphemus with his real name, out of pride, on the way out. That's not what saves his people — it's what dooms them. Polyphemus's curse to his father Poseidon is the reason nearly everything afterward goes wrong, and every single one of Odysseus's roughly 600 men is dead by the time he reaches Calypso's island. A raid on the Cicones early in the voyage — unprovoked, for plunder — costs 72 more men to a counterattack he brought on himself. Homer's *Odyssey* is, if anything, unusually explicit that Odysseus's own choices cost his men their lives, not that his rule-breaking protected them. "Helps and protects," the chart's definition of Good, is a genuine stretch for a character defined at least as much by pride and deception as by care for his crew.

**Recommendation:** move Odysseus from Chaotic Good to **Lawful Neutral** on the artifact, matching WotC's own canon. If you'd rather keep the Homer-only framing distinct from official D&D lore (a defensible editorial choice, not a factual error either way), the fallback worth considering is **Chaotic Neutral** — cunning and self-interested rather than rule-bound, but still not "Good" given the pride-driven harm to his own crew. Either way, Chaotic *Good* specifically is the one combination the evidence doesn't support. This doesn't touch the other eight characters on the chart — nothing here questioned Penelope, Nausicaa, or anyone else.

## Sherlock — What are the facts?

**The artifact as it currently exists.** The chart ("The Odyssey: A D&D Alignment Chart") is a 9-box grid crossing Lawful/Neutral/Chaotic against Good/Neutral/Evil, with the axes defined on the chart itself: Lawful = "Values order and duty," Neutral = "Judges each situation," Chaotic = "Values freedom and impulse"; Good = "Helps and protects," Evil = "Hurts or exploits." Odysseus is placed at **Chaotic Good**, captioned "Breaks rules to save his people and return home." The other eight boxes: Penelope (Lawful Good), Nausicaa (Neutral Good), Nestor (Lawful Neutral), Circe (True Neutral), Aeolus (Chaotic Neutral), Poseidon (Lawful Evil), The Suitors (Neutral Evil), Polyphemus (Chaotic Evil). A footer reads: "Alignment is a compass, not a cage. It describes usual tendencies — not every choice."

**Official D&D canon places Odysseus differently.** Wizards of the Coast's *Complete Scoundrel* (2007, by Mike McArtor and F. Wesley Schneider) is cited, per the D&D Lore Wiki (Fandom), as explicitly naming real-world/fictional example characters for each alignment — one of the only official WotC sourcebooks to do this. Under **Lawful Neutral**: "James Bond, Odysseus, and Sanjuro from *Yojimbo* are Lawful Neutral." The wiki's own definition of Lawful Neutral: "typically believes strongly in Lawful concepts such as honor, order, rules and tradition, and often follows a personal code... their moral considerations come a distant second to what their code, tradition or law dictates." Direct attempts to find a specific WotC discussion of *why* Odysseus fits this (beyond the source-list citation itself) came up empty — the sourcebook's own reasoning for the pairing wasn't independently located this pass.

**Homer's text, as established in the 2026-07-25 report and confirmed here:**
- Odysseus's defining Homeric epithet is *polymetis* — "of many wiles/counsels" — describing cunning and strategic thinking, not impulse.
- He waits 10 years at Troy, then 10 more getting home; refuses Calypso's offer of immortality to keep pursuing the return; has himself bound to the mast rather than act on impulse toward the Sirens.
- He spends the entire second half of the poem in disguise (Athena-altered, presenting as a beggar), gathering information patiently in his own hall before acting — the opposite of impulsive.
- The single highest-consequence rule-break in the poem — taunting the blinded Polyphemus with his real name — is explicitly driven by pride ("an act of pride, not necessity," per the 2026-07-25 report's own Euclid analysis), and directly causes Poseidon's curse, which the poem treats as the cause of nearly everything that goes wrong afterward, including the deaths of the entire crew.
- The raid on the Cicones, early in the return voyage, is unprovoked plunder-seeking against a Trojan ally not actively hostile at that moment — the resulting counterattack kills 72 men (6 per ship × 12 ships), the first crew losses of the voyage.
- Extensive deception continues even after any practical danger has passed — Odysseus keeps his identity hidden from his own father, Laertes, near the very end of the poem, apparently by habit/character rather than necessity.
- The ending is violent at scale: 108 suitors killed, plus 12 serving-women hanged and the goatherd Melanthius mutilated — restoration of rightful rule, but not a gentle one.

**The 2026 Nolan film.** Christopher Nolan's *The Odyssey* (Matt Damon as Odysseus, released July 17, 2026) is confirmed real — box office and cast details corroborated across multiple outlets. Nolan has described Odysseus, in interviews around the film, as "an amazing strategist, and a very wily person," explicitly citing Emily Wilson's 2017 translation — the translation famous for rendering the poem's opening description of Odysseus (*polytropos*) as "complicated," a deliberate emphasis on moral and characterological complexity over simple heroism. Detailed professional reviews of Damon's specific performance and character choices weren't comprehensively available this pass; the director's own stated framing was the strongest available signal.

## Euclid — What must be fundamentally true?

Apply the chart's own axis definitions to the evidence, not a generic sense of "hero" or "villain." On Law/Chaos: the chart defines Chaotic as valuing "freedom and impulse." Odysseus's dominant, poem-spanning trait is the opposite — patience, calculation, disguise, and a personal code (get home, restore his household) pursued with discipline across two full decades. That's much closer to the chart's own definition of Lawful ("order and duty") than Chaotic, provided "order and duty" is read as Odysseus's own code rather than obedience to external authority — which is exactly the nuance the Lawful Neutral definition captures ("their moral considerations come a distant second to what their code, tradition or law dictates").

On Good/Evil: the chart's caption for Odysseus — "breaks rules to save his people" — doesn't survive contact with the text. His single most consequential rule-break (the Polyphemus taunt) is the direct cause of his people's destruction, not their salvation. A character whose defining transgression kills his own crew, who raids an uninvolved party for plunder, and who keeps deceiving his own family well past the point of necessity, is not well described by "helps and protects." He is genuinely oriented toward his household's restoration — that's real and shouldn't be erased — but the path there runs through enough self-interest, pride, and collateral harm that "Good" overstates it. "Neutral" (committed to those close to him, not to a general altruism) fits the textual evidence better.

Both axes point the same direction independently derived from the text — and that direction happens to match WotC's own official classification exactly. That convergence, from two independent methods (first-principles textual analysis and citing the publisher's own canon), is stronger evidence than either alone.

## Popper — How could we be wrong?

1. **"Lawful" is a real stretch given the Cicones raid and the Polyphemus taunt.** Both are impulsive, arguably reckless acts — raiding for plunder without provocation, and taunting a defeated enemy out of pure pride when silence cost nothing. These look more Chaotic than Lawful on their face, and they're not minor footnotes — one of them is the hinge the entire second half of the plot turns on.
2. **WotC's own sourcebook citation might be a shallow pop-culture pairing, not deep textual analysis.** *Complete Scoundrel* is a rules supplement citing real-world characters as quick-reference shorthand for players, not a work of literary criticism — the reasoning behind "Odysseus = Lawful Neutral" wasn't independently found this pass. It's possible the actual WotC writers were thinking of a pop-culture-shorthand version of Odysseus (calculating antihero, "the Bond of antiquity") rather than engaging closely with Homer's text the way this report has.
3. **Chaotic Neutral is a plausible alternative that wasn't fully ruled out.** Self-interested, cunning, unbound by rules when they're inconvenient (the Cicones raid, the Polyphemus taunt, extensive lying) — this fits at least as well as Lawful Neutral for the Law/Chaos axis specifically, and this report's argument for "Lawful" leans heavily on reading his personal code as equivalent to the chart's "order and duty," which is an interpretive choice, not the only reasonable one.
4. **The Nolan film's specific characterization wasn't actually verified in detail** — only the director's own framing in interviews, not a close reading of how the film itself portrays Odysseus's choices scene by scene. Rick has seen the film and this report hasn't independently confirmed what in it specifically triggered his doubt about the chart.

## Seldon — What is likely to happen next?

Addressing each point: (1) is real and shouldn't be minimized — but the chart's own footer states alignment "describes usual tendencies — not every choice," and Odysseus's usual mode across ~20 years of narrated behavior is patience and calculation, with the Cicones and Polyphemus incidents standing out precisely *because* they're exceptions he never repeats (he never again taunts a defeated enemy or raids without cause) — consistent with a fundamentally Lawful character who has identifiable, narratively-significant lapses, not a fundamentally Chaotic one. (2) is a fair caveat to carry forward rather than resolve — this report cites WotC's placement as strong corroborating evidence, not as proof on its own, and says so explicitly rather than treating "the publisher said so" as the whole argument. (3) is genuinely unresolved: Chaotic Neutral remains a defensible second answer, which is exactly why Bradlee's recommendation above offers it as an explicit fallback rather than presenting Lawful Neutral as the only possible fix. (4) is a real gap — if Rick wants the film's specific character choices checked against the book's, that's a natural, well-scoped follow-up question for a future engagement, not something to guess at here.

No forecast is warranted — this is a question of textual/interpretive correctness, not a forward-looking uncertainty.
