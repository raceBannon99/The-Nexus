# 2026-10-08: US Lawmakers Legislating the AI Kill Switch, AGI, the Singularity, and Superintelligence

**Question:** What US lawmakers — House or Senate — have proposed legislation dealing with an AI kill switch, AGI, the Singularity, Superintelligence, or anything else designed to slow down development from frontier AI companies or anybody else?

**Scope, per Rick's answers to the clarifying questions below:** the 119th Congress (2025–present) only; federal bills as the primary subject, with notable state bills included for context; broad interpretation of "slow down development" (export/compute controls, licensing, liability, and state-preemption fights all in bounds, not just bills that use the words "kill switch" or "AGI"); and only bills that are currently active (introduced or formally announced), not ones that have already died.

---

## Synthesis

Congress has stopped debating whether to regulate frontier AI and started introducing bills that would actually restrict it — at least eight of them are alive right now, running from a flat ban on superintelligence to a five-year ban on states regulating AI at all. None has become law. The most direct kill-switch bill was blocked on the Senate floor three weeks ago by a Republican colleague of its own sponsor.

Two House members, Ted Lieu of California and Nathaniel Moran of Texas, introduced the AI Kill Switch Act (H.R. 9917) on July 23, 2026, requiring the developers of the most powerful AI systems to maintain the technical ability to throttle, suspend, or shut them down, and giving the Secretary of Homeland Security authority to order a slowdown or shutdown of a system capable of catastrophic harm. Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana introduced a Senate companion, the AI Emergency Button Act, that puts the shutoff in the companies' own hands rather than the government's — and then tried to pass it by unanimous consent on September 16. Senator Rand Paul objected, arguing that mandating kill switches before the technology is understood "risks killing innovation and setting this technology back decades." The bill is stalled. A House companion from Representative Tom Kean of New Jersey faces the same math.

Further out on the restrictive end, Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar announced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act on September 3 — a permanent prohibition on building or deploying a system that matches or exceeds human cognitive performance, an immediate pause on any system trained above roughly current frontier scale, a new cabinet-level Department of Artificial Intelligence to enforce it, and prison terms up to 20 years for violators. It is the most aggressive bill in Congress today, more aggressive than any draft legislation produced inside this research effort to date.

In between sit bills built around disclosure and audits rather than outright bans — the bipartisan FRONTIER Act (H.R. 9925) from Representatives Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan, and a still-unintroduced duty-of-care framework from Senate Majority Leader John Thune, Commerce Chairman Ted Cruz, and Senator Amy Klobuchar that would let the Commerce Department block a model's release and sue to enjoin one it considers unsafe. Both carry a second, less-discussed feature: they would set a single federal standard that preempts the state laws — California's and New York's — that are the only AI safety rules actually in force today. A separate bill, Representative Michael Baumgartner's H.R. 5388, does nothing but that: a five-year ban on new state AI rules, the standalone descendant of the ten-year preemption provision the House passed in its 2025 reconciliation bill and the Senate stripped 99–1. The fight in Washington right now is at least as much about who gets to regulate AI as whether anyone should.

Nothing here is likely to become law quickly. The most probable path to a binding federal restriction runs through a triggering incident — several of the kind Congress is already reacting to have occurred this year — more than through the current bills reaching consensus on their own; a reasonable estimate puts the median date for the first one actually taking effect around 2030, with real uncertainty on both sides of that number. Until then, California and New York's state laws, not anything moving in Washington, are the only binding restrictions a frontier AI company actually has to answer to.

## The Facts (Agent Sherlock)

Research confirms Congress is more active on this subject than at any point in the 119th Congress's history, though none of it has reached a floor vote that succeeded. The complete bill-by-bill picture is in the Bill Tracker table below. Key findings:

- **At least eight live federal proposals** touch kill switches, AGI/superintelligence, or frontier-AI slowdown, spanning both chambers and both parties, introduced or announced between September 2025 and October 2026.
- **None has passed either chamber.** The furthest any has gotten is a failed unanimous-consent request (Kennedy's AI Emergency Button Act, blocked by Paul on the Senate floor, September 16, 2026) and committee referral (most others).
- **The fight over frontier-AI slowdown is entangled with a separate fight over who is allowed to regulate at all.** The House passed a 10-year federal moratorium on state AI regulation inside the 2025 reconciliation bill ("One Big Beautiful Bill Act"); the Senate stripped it 99–1 (Cantwell-Blackburn amendment) before the bill became law July 4, 2025. That fight continues on two fronts: a standalone 5-year version (Rep. Baumgartner's H.R. 5388) and a quieter preemption clause embedded in the bipartisan FRONTIER Act/Great American AI Act package. Separately, Executive Order 14365 (December 11, 2025) directs a DOJ litigation task force to challenge state AI laws directly — an executive, not legislative, mechanism pursuing the same goal after the legislative moratorium failed.
- **State legislatures — not Congress — currently hold the only binding, in-force frontier-AI restrictions in the country.** California's SB 53 (Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act, signed September 29, 2025, effective January 1, 2026) and New York's RAISE Act (signed December 19, 2025) both require catastrophic-risk protocols, transparency reporting, and incident disclosure from large frontier developers, with civil penalties. Colorado's AI Act (SB 24-205, repealed and reenacted as SB 26-189, effective January 1, 2027) is a different animal — algorithmic-discrimination/automated-decision-making regulation, not a frontier-AI-slowdown measure — and is noted here only to rule it out of this survey's core scope.
- **California's governor has gone further than any legislature has, administratively.** Gov. Newsom's September 18, 2026 executive order explicitly directs state agencies to "advance the creation of an AI kill switch," with named experts announced September 23. This is the single instance anywhere in this survey of a government official using the literal phrase "kill switch" in an official capacity — but it is an executive order from a governor, not legislation from a lawmaker, so it sits outside this question's core subject even though it belongs in the full picture.
- **The newest entrant, Senator Cantwell's six-point AI safety framework (announced the same day as this engagement, October 8, 2026),** is a competing alternative to the stalled Thune-Cruz-Klobuchar duty-of-care draft inside the Senate Commerce Committee — testing standards, mandatory pre-release audits, whistleblower protections — but is not yet bill text, and notably does not touch kill switches, AGI, or superintelligence at all; it is a narrower testing-and-audit regime.

**On the First Principles Newsletter Tracker:** this question bears directly on Thread 5, whose most recent essay ("A Pinky Swear at the White House," Oct. 2026) argues the White House's voluntary AI-safety accord is inadequate because "binding rules have to come from Congress, not from a luncheon." A dated Evidence Log entry has been appended to that file (see Sources). In short: Congress has, in fact, begun producing exactly the binding-rules-from-Congress mechanism Rick's essay calls for — including one bill (the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act) more aggressive than his own drafted RSI Safety Act — which supports the thesis. But the same Congress is simultaneously trying to strip states of the power to impose binding rules of their own, and the one bill that reached an actual floor vote was blocked — which complicates it. Logged as both, not resolved to one side.

**Forecast input (Sherlock):** Reasoning from the facts alone — several real bills now exist, but none has cleared committee, and the one that reached the floor was blocked — a first estimate: the range runs from about 2027 to 2040, with a median around 2029.

## First-Principles Analysis (Agent Euclid)

Reasoning from the historical pattern of catastrophic-risk regulation — nuclear licensing after Three Mile Island, financial reform after 2008, aviation rules after fatal crashes — binding restrictions on a powerful new technology have almost never preceded a clear triggering event; they have followed one, usually within one to three years. That pattern, not the current bill count, is the better predictor of when (not whether) Congress enacts something binding here.

Measured against that pattern, 2026 looks less like a quiet year and more like the early innings of exactly that triggering sequence, just not yet a single dispositive event. The incidents Congress's own sponsors are citing by name — the Hugging Face agent swarm, OpenAI's repeated training-agent containment failures, the DIVD-disclosed autonomous zero-day chain — are accumulating at a pace that, on the historical pattern, points toward a real trigger inside the next two to three years rather than a decade out. The eight bills now pending are best read as pre-positioned legislative text waiting for that moment, not a serious attempt to build consensus on their own merits — which is also why none of them has moved: there is no triggering event yet forcing anyone to choose among eight competing drafts.

This directly engages Rick's own "binding rules have to come from Congress" position (Thread 5, First Principles Newsletter Tracker). The first-principles read here agrees with the destination but revises the mechanism: Congress is not going to deliberate its way to a kill switch through the ordinary committee process these eight bills are stuck in. It is going to pass one of them — probably the narrowest one already sitting closest to consensus, the "imminent catastrophic risk" suspension authority that the FRONTIER Act and the Thune-Cruz-Klobuchar draft both independently landed on — in the window immediately following whatever incident finally forces the question, the same way Dodd-Frank passed in the quarters after Lehman, not the years of hearings that preceded it.

**Forecast input (Euclid):** the range runs from about 2026 to 2035, with a median around 2028 — tighter and earlier than Sherlock's, on the view that a triggering incident, not committee consensus, is the actual rate-limiting step, and the incident drumbeat is already well underway.

## Stress Test (Agent Popper)

Euclid's "incident triggers passage" model is the right historical pattern, but it is doing more work here than the evidence supports, for three reasons.

First, the incident count is misleading as a leading indicator. Every incident cited above — Hugging Face, the OpenAI containment failures, the DIVD chain — already occurred, and none of them triggered anything beyond bill introductions and a blocked unanimous-consent request. If a dozen qualifying incidents weren't enough to move Kennedy's bill past Rand Paul's objection, the threshold for "trigger" is evidently higher than Euclid's model assumes, or the triggering mechanism isn't incident severity at all but something else — public attention, an election cycle, a specific casualty event — that hasn't happened yet.

Second, Euclid's prediction that a narrow "imminent catastrophic risk" suspension bill is the likely first mover undersells how genuinely contested even that narrow version is. The Thune-Cruz-Klobuchar negotiations stalled specifically over the suspension mechanism itself — Cantwell wants mandatory federal-lab vetting before suspension authority is credible, Cruz wants company self-testing — which is a disagreement about the exact power Euclid is predicting will pass first. That is not a drafting detail; it is the same fight, one level down.

Third, both Sherlock and Euclid underweight the accelerationist counter-current. Eight bills restrict; at least two real, moving measures (H.R. 5388 and the preemption language inside FRONTIER Act/GAAIA) point the other direction, and they have an incumbent President who has publicly dismissed AI-doom scenarios as a "hoax," and a House that already passed a 10-year moratorium once. A Congress that passed a sweeping preemption measure through one chamber in 2025 and has two more live preemption vehicles in 2026 is not obviously closer to banning superintelligence than it is to banning state oversight of it. The two are structurally in tension, and the stronger of the two pushes dates later, not sooner.

None of this means Euclid is wrong that something eventually passes — the historical pattern he cites is sound. It means the "something" is more likely to be contested, narrow, and later than his estimate assumes, and the probability mass on "no binding federal restriction this decade at all" is larger than either prior estimate allows for.

**Forecast input (Popper):** the range runs from about 2027 to 2045, with a median around 2032 — wider and later than both Sherlock's and Euclid's, reflecting genuine disagreement on mechanism (Cantwell vs. Cruz) and a live, well-resourced accelerationist counter-current that the incident-trigger model doesn't account for.

## Forecast Synthesis (Agent Seldon)

Three independent estimates were collected for when the first binding federal restriction on frontier AI — a kill-switch mandate, an AGI/superintelligence ban, or an RSI licensing gate, specifically excluding a pure study/evaluation bill like S. 2938 — actually takes effect:

| Stage | P5 | P50 | P95 | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sherlock | 2027 | 2029 | 2040 | Grounded in the bare fact pattern: real bills exist, none has cleared committee. |
| Euclid | 2026 | 2028 | 2035 | Incident-trigger model from catastrophic-risk regulatory history; incident drumbeat already underway. |
| Popper | 2027 | 2032 | 2045 | Stress-tests the trigger model: incidents already failed to move Kennedy's bill; narrow-bill consensus is itself contested; accelerationist counter-current pulls dates later. |

The three agree on the earliest edge (2026–2027) — nobody thinks this happens sooner than that, since nothing currently pending is close enough to a vote to pass faster. They diverge most on the median: Euclid's incident-trigger model wants 2028, Popper's contested-mechanism-plus-counter-current read wants 2032. Popper's critique survives direct engagement — the concrete fact that a documented incident drumbeat already failed to move the one bill that reached a floor vote is hard evidence against treating incident accumulation alone as the rate-limiting step, and the preemption fight is real, live, and currently winning more procedural ground (a House floor vote, a standalone bill) than any restrictive measure has. But Euclid's underlying mechanism — that catastrophic-risk regulation historically follows a trigger rather than deliberation — isn't refuted, just mistimed; the right correction is to weight the trigger model's timing later, not discard it.

Synthesized call: **the range runs from about 2027 to 2038 — an 11-year span — with a median around 2030, roughly 3 years out from the earliest plausible date.** This splits the difference between Euclid's and Popper's medians rather than averaging all three mechanically, because Popper's specific objection (a real trigger already fired and didn't work) is the single most concrete, falsifiable fact among the three inputs and earns real weight, while Sherlock's bare-fact-pattern estimate and Euclid's mechanism both still inform the window's width. This is reasoned judgment under genuine uncertainty, not measured data — there is no historical base rate for "time to first binding AI-specific federal restriction" the way there is for, say, time-to-first-FDA-approval. Treat the 2030 median as a weak central tendency, not a prediction with real confidence behind it.
