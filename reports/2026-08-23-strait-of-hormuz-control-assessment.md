# Strait of Hormuz Control Assessment — Oil Flow, Losses, and Casualties Since Operation Epic Fury

**Question:** Assess control of the Strait of Hormuz from the beginning of the Iran war on 28 February 2026 until today, using daily oil-flow volume (barrels/day) as the key metric, and also accounting for ships sunk on all sides, drones shot down, and people killed.

## Bradlee's Synthesis

Six months into the war (CENTCOM Day 177), nobody controls the Strait of Hormuz in the traditional military sense — but the underlying trend increasingly favors the U.S.-led coalition, not Iran, and oil-flow data is the clearest single proxy for that shift.

Iran's conventional navy is effectively destroyed: the frigate Sabalan, forward-basing ship Makran, both Mowj-class corvettes, and the frigate Dena (torpedoed by a U.S. submarine while returning from exercises, with roughly 180 crew aboard) are gone, alongside CENTCOM's claimed — though not independently itemized — total of 60 vessels sunk in the war's opening two weeks. What survives is the IRGC's irregular fast-boat, mine, and one-way-attack-drone force, which cannot contest the U.S. Navy militarily but doesn't need to: cheap, hard-to-fully-suppress harassment has kept the strait a contested, underused chokepoint for six months despite Iran's fleet being gone.

Oil flow is the best evidence for who's actually winning that contest, precisely because both sides have an incentive to spin the other metrics. It has moved through three distinct phases since Feb 28: a low of ~2.3 million barrels/day through the pre-MOU blockade era (against a pre-war baseline of 21.6 million), a rebound to a 6.1-million-barrel average during the 60-day Islamabad MOU window (June 18–Aug 17), and a disputed current figure since the MOU's Aug 17 expiration — Iran claims total closure, the U.S. claims 8–15 million barrels/day, and the more credible independent middle sits around 4–7 million (Kpler, JPMorgan). The trend inside that dispute matters more than any single number: over 80% of transits in the past two weeks have used the Omani route or gone dark rather than Iran's claimed-controlled central lane, and Iran — despite the MOU's expiration technically permitting it — hasn't been able to resume collecting tolls. That's a real, measurable loss of practical control, not just a rhetorical one.

The human and material cost is asymmetric and only partly attributable to Iran. At least 18 seafarers have been confirmed killed in attacks on commercial shipping since Feb 28 (International Maritime Organization), the large majority in Iranian attacks — but U.S. forces have acknowledged roughly half a dozen strikes on shipping themselves, including a June 10 attack that killed three Indian seafarers. No comprehensive public tally of drones shot down exists; individual CENTCOM-confirmed intercepts (four in one incident, "all" in another, 19 during the related strikes on Oman) are documented but don't sum to a verified campaign total, and that gap is stated here rather than papered over with an invented number. This is deliberately scoped to the Hormuz shipping war — the broader war's total death toll (credibly estimated in the thousands, driven mostly by strikes on Iranian and Lebanese territory) is a different, much larger number covered elsewhere and not what "control of the strait" is asking about.

**How to apply this:** treat any single flow or control claim from either government with real skepticism, weight the independent trade-intelligence data (Kpler, Lloyd's List) over both sides' official statements, and expect the picture to keep shifting — Oman-Iran talks are underway outside U.S. involvement, and Washington's threatened "economic D-Day" sanctions package (details due Monday, Aug 24) could reset the dynamic again within days of this report's publication.

## Sherlock: What Are the Facts?

### Background: how the naval/economic war unfolded

A full-scale war between the United States, Israel, and Iran began 28 February 2026 (Operation Epic Fury / Operation Roaring Lion), with Iranian Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and senior IRGC/military leadership reported killed within hours. Iran responded by declaring the Strait of Hormuz closed to US/Israel-linked shipping and began an asymmetric campaign of attacks on merchant vessels. What followed was not a single, static blockade but six overlapping phases, each with its own control dynamic and its own oil-flow number:

1. **Iranian closure-by-attack (Feb 28 – Apr 8):** Iran declares closure (Mar 2), claims "complete control" (Mar 4). The US destroys most of Iran's conventional navy in response (see Losses tables below) and begins its own aerial campaign against Iranian naval and mine-laying assets (Mar 17–20). Tanker traffic drops toward zero; by Apr 4, roughly 20,000 mariners and 2,000 ships are stranded in the Persian Gulf.
2. **First ceasefire attempt (Apr 8 – Apr 13):** A temporary ceasefire is agreed Apr 8, intended to reopen the strait — but within days the US imposes its *own* naval blockade of Iranian ports (Apr 13), so the strait moves from one-sided closure to a dueling-blockade dynamic rather than opening.
3. **Dueling blockades (Apr 13 – Jun 18):** US blockade of Iranian ports in force; Operation Project Freedom (a US escort mission) launches May 4 and is paused May 6 amid negotiation progress. Kpler's tracking puts the average flow for this stretch (Apr 1–Jun 17) at **2.3 million barrels/day** — the low point of the war.
4. **Islamabad MOU / blockade lift (Jun 17/18 – Aug 17):** Trump and Iranian President Pezeshkian sign the Islamabad Memorandum Jun 17; the US lifts its blockade and Iran commits to safe, no-charge passage for a 60-day window. Flow rebounds to a **6.1 million barrel/day** average over the full window (374 million barrels total, per Kpler) — nearly triple the pre-MOU rate but still only ~40% of the ~15 million barrels/day that transited the strait daily in 2025. More than half of the MOU-period volume moved in the agreement's first three weeks; by the end, per Kpler's Emmanuel Belostrino, "the flow was thinner, darker and re-accumulating behind the chokepoint." Iran itself re-declared a closure Jun 20 (citing alleged Israeli violations) even while the MOU nominally held, and the interim truce broke down entirely by Jul 8, with the US blockade reinstated Jul 14–15 — meaning the *practical* MOU-era safe-passage arrangement lasted closer to four weeks than sixty days, even though the diplomatic 60-day clock itself didn't formally expire until Aug 17.
5. **Second blockade, MOU expiration (Jul 14 – Aug 17):** Blockade back in force; UKMTO raises the threat level to "severe" after renewed tanker attacks in early July; by Aug 11 the US has redirected roughly 55 vessels since the blockade resumed. The MOU's 60-day diplomatic window lapses Aug 17 without a follow-on deal.
6. **Post-MOU, contested present (Aug 17 – today):** No new ceasefire is in force. Both governments claim control while independent shipping data shows a split reality — see Euclid's and Popper's sections below.

## Euclid: What Must Be Fundamentally True?

Military "control" of a chokepoint has historically meant one side can guarantee safe passage for itself while reliably denying or threatening it for the other. Neither side can do that here. Iran cannot stop the US Navy from operating in or near the strait — its conventional navy has been effectively destroyed (IRIS Dena, Sabalan, both Mowj-class corvettes, Makran, and, per CENTCOM's own claim, a cumulative 60 vessels in the war's opening two weeks). But the US and its coalition partners cannot guarantee unmolested passage for merchant shipping either, since scattered IRGC asymmetric attacks — mines, one-way-attack drones, fast boats — continue sporadically regardless of how the conventional naval battle has gone.

That means "control" isn't one question but at least three separable ones, and they currently have different answers:

1. **Military/naval balance of forces** — who would win a symmetric naval engagement. Decisively the US/coalition; Iran's conventional fleet is gone.
2. **Freedom of navigation in practice** — can ships transit safely and predictably. Neither side; six months in, traffic remains a fraction (roughly 20%, by the most recent UKMTO-based count) of the pre-war average, regardless of who is nominally "in charge."
3. **Economic leverage** — who profits from or taxes the flow. Iran has largely lost this since the MOU's expiration: despite technically regaining the right to charge tolls, it hasn't been able to collect them, and most traffic now avoids its claimed-controlled central route entirely.

The first-principles read is that sustained asymmetric denial — mines, drones, small boats, and the insurance/psychological effect of unpredictable attacks — is structurally cheaper to *maintain* than it is for a superior conventional force to fully *suppress*. That's why a conventionally defeated Iranian navy still exerts a real chokepoint effect nearly six months into the war; it's consistent with the historical pattern of chokepoint/tanker warfare (e.g., the 1980s Iran–Iraq "Tanker War"), not a temporary anomaly specific to this conflict.

**Net answer:** as of Aug 23, 2026, no single party controls the Strait of Hormuz in the traditional sense. The US/coalition holds clear military dominance and has visibly eroded Iran's ability to enforce its claimed closure. Iran retains just enough residual asymmetric capability to keep the strait contested, high-risk, and severely underutilized rather than fully open. Oil flow — currently somewhere between roughly a fifth and a third of the pre-war rate, depending on whose estimate is used — is the most honest single proxy for "who controls the strait," because unlike rhetoric, it's the one thing neither government can fully spin away.

## Popper: How Could We Be Wrong?

Five challenges to the draft as it stood after Euclid:

1. **"Neither side controls it" may be a false balance that understates a real trend.** The evidence — >80% of transits now using the Omani route or going dark, Iran unable to collect tolls despite regaining the legal right to, Kpler's own analyst stating Iran "has at least partially lost control" — points to a measurable *shift* toward coalition dominance over roughly the past month, not a static 50/50 stalemate. A reader taking Euclid's framing as a permanent judgment would be misled.
2. **The oil-flow metric itself may be propaganda-laundering, not measurement.** Iran claims total closure (serving its "resistance" narrative and its threat to cut "every drop" if neighbors defect); the US claims 8–15 million barrels/day (serving Trump's "total control" claim). Both are self-interested parties reporting on their own success. Presenting a "4–15 million barrel" range as if it were an honest measurement-uncertainty band risks treating two propaganda claims as legitimate data points bracketing the truth, rather than what they actually are: outliers around a smaller independent estimate.
3. **The "18 seafarers killed" figure obscures a real accountability question.** Some of those deaths are attributable to US/coalition fire (the Jun 10 Palau-tanker strike that killed 3 Indian seafarers), not only to Iran. A report titled around "control" — implicitly a two-sided framing — shouldn't fold "who died" into a single unattributed number.
4. **CENTCOM's "60 Iranian vessels sunk" is being repeated too uncritically.** It's a Pentagon self-reported aggregate from the war's opening two weeks, never independently itemized ship-by-ship — unlike the specifically named and separately corroborated losses (Dena, Sabalan, the two Mowj-class corvettes, Makran, the 16 minelayers). Citing it in the same breath as those named losses risks lending it more credibility than it has earned.
5. **The Wikipedia-sourced timeline has at least one demonstrable error, which should lower trust in its uncorroborated entries.** Wikipedia's "2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis" article lists the Minoan Dignity as *sunk*; the more carefully dated, directly sourced Al Jazeera account of the same incident (Aug 20, citing INTERCARGO) describes it only as struck, with one crew fatality — not sunk. If the aggregation has one confirmed error, entries that trace only to lower-tier sources (e.g., hormuzstraitmonitor.com's uncorroborated "20 million barrels in 24 hours" and "1% baseline" figures) deserve real skepticism, not automatic inclusion.
