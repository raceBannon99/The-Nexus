# When Did "Black Hat Hacker" and "White Hat Hacker" Become Common Terms?

**Question:** No single person can be credited with coining the black-hat/white-hat hacker terminology — but can we narrow down *when* it became common usage, digging into phreaker-era sources (2600, Phrack, old Usenet) that might predate the Oxford English Dictionary's 1990 citation?

## Sherlock — What are the facts?

**No usage predating 1990 was found, despite specifically checking the sources most likely to carry it.** Three targeted checks came back negative or inconclusive:

- **Phrack's most famous 1986 document, "The Conscience of a Hacker" ("The Hacker Manifesto") by Loyd Blankenship ("The Mentor")** — the canonical early text of hacker self-identity, written after his arrest and published in Phrack Issue 7 — does not use "black hat" or "white hat" terminology anywhere in its text.
- **Phrack's own archive** (`archives.phrack.org/issues/`) lists issues 1–72 but the directory listing carries no dates or searchable index, making a systematic sweep for early "black hat"/"white hat" usage impractical without opening each issue individually — not attempted exhaustively this pass (see Popper).
- **Old Usenet (alt.2600, alt.hackers, founded 1990)** — Google Groups' modern search interface has degraded significantly for pre-1995 Usenet archives (a widely noted problem; one Hacker News discussion found in this research bluntly called Google's handling of the old Usenet archive "unforgivable"), and searches for the exact phrases returned only modern, unrelated Google Groups content rather than dated historical posts. This is a genuine tooling limitation, not a negative result — see Popper.

**What is confirmed, with real dates:**

- The **general** "villain wears black, hero wears white" convention comes from 1950s Western films — this predates any hacker-specific usage by decades and is not in dispute.
- The **Oxford English Dictionary** records "white hat" in **computing slang starting in 1990** — the earliest specifically-dated citation found for the term's use in a computing/hacking context, from either this pass or the prior conversation.
- **October 3, 1999** — Bruce Gottlieb's article "HacK, CouNterHaCk," published in *The New York Times Magazine*, describes members of L0pht Heavy Industries (a prominent Boston hacker collective) using both terms in a mainstream national publication: some members characterized as "black hat hackers," the group collectively described elsewhere as "white-hat hackers." This is the earliest specific, dated, mainstream-press usage found of both terms together in the same hacking context.
- **Also in 1999** — the term "grey hat" enters print for the first time, coined in the context of L0pht's own ambiguous positioning (documented on Wikipedia's "Grey hat" page and corroborated by multiple secondary sources, including academic literature on gray-hat hacking law). This detail matters more than it looks: a joke/derivative term like "grey hat" — splitting the difference between two existing categories — only makes sense to an audience for whom "black hat" and "white hat" are *already* familiar, established terms. Its 1999 appearance is indirect but solid evidence that the two-term dichotomy was common currency in hacker and security circles well before that point, not something newly coined that year.
- **January 2000** — L0pht Heavy Industries merged with startup @stake, completing what secondary sources describe as the group's transition from underground organization to "white hat" computer-security company — a sign the term had become a legitimate business/industry label, not just subculture slang, right at the turn of the millennium.
- **1997** — Jeff Moss founded the Black Hat Briefings conference using the term as an already-recognizable, marketable name (established in yesterday's origin-history report) — consistent with the term being common enough by the mid-1990s that naming a professional conference after it made sense as a branding choice, though (as that report also found) no source documents Moss's own specific reasoning for the choice.

## Euclid — What must be fundamentally true?

Three structural facts, reasoned from the evidence above rather than assumed:

- **A term's *documented* first appearance and its *actual* first appearance are different things, and the gap between them is usually largest for subculture slang.** The OED's 1990 citation is the earliest *someone bothered to write it down in a source that survived and got indexed* — it is not, and cannot be treated as, proof that the term didn't exist earlier in spoken or informally-circulated form within BBS/phreaker culture. Slang routinely precedes its first lexicographic citation by years; this is true of "hacker" itself, whose 1963 MIT *The Tech* citation is understood to reflect an already-circulating campus usage, not a coinage event. The honest position is that 1990 is the earliest *documented* floor, not necessarily the actual origin point.
- **The "grey hat" 1999 evidence is a stronger signal of established usage than the OED's 1990 citation is of first usage**, precisely because a derivative/joke term requires an already-shared reference point to land as a joke at all. This means the real, useful claim isn't "the term appeared in 1990" — it's "the term was common enough, in a subculture spanning both underground BBS/phreaker circles and the emerging professional security industry, that by 1999 people were confidently building new vocabulary on top of it in front of a *New York Times* readership that needed no explanation of the base terms."
- **The absence of a pre-1990 citation in this pass's research is evidence of a tooling limit, not evidence of absence.** Phrack's archive has no searchable full-text index; the 1986 Hacker Manifesto is one document among dozens from that era, not a representative sample; and Google's own Usenet archive is acknowledged (including by outside observers) to have degraded for exactly this kind of historical search. A clean "not found before 1990" claim would overstate what this research actually established.

## Popper — How could we be wrong?

1. **This pass did not exhaustively search Phrack or 2600's archives — it spot-checked the single most famous document and gave up on a systematic sweep once the archive proved to have no searchable index.** There are 72 Phrack issues and roughly four decades of 2600 back issues; checking one document (the Hacker Manifesto) and concluding "not found in the earliest, most famous text" is a much weaker claim than "not found after checking the corpus." If Rick wants a stronger answer, someone would need to either manually work through early-1990s Phrack issues one at a time, or find a service with actual full-text search across the archive (neither was available to this research pass).
2. **The Usenet search failure is a real gap, not a minor caveat.** alt.hackers and alt.2600 are exactly the kind of forum where this slang would have circulated earliest and most informally, predating any print zine's editorial lag — and this is precisely the source this pass could not search effectively. Treat the "no pre-1990 usage found" finding as unverified rather than refuted, not as a negative result with real evidentiary weight.
3. **The 1990 OED citation and the 1999 NYT citation might not be measuring comparable things.** The OED entry likely draws from written, published sources meeting lexicographic citation standards; the NYT piece is a single journalist's usage in a specific article. It's possible "white hat" was already print-documented well before 1999 in sources the OED simply didn't index for its dictionary entry (industry trade publications, early infosec conference proceedings, etc.) — this report didn't attempt a systematic search of that middle tier of sources (neither underground zine nor major national newspaper).
4. **Euclid's reasoning about "grey hat implies prior familiarity" is sound logic but still an inference, not a direct citation.** It's a reasonable and probably correct argument, but it's still Nexus's own analytical reasoning layered on top of the Wikipedia/secondary-source claim about grey hat's 1999 origin — not something a primary source states explicitly ("black hat and white hat were already well established by the time we coined grey hat").

## Seldon — What is likely to happen next?

Addressing Popper's four points: points 1 and 2 (incomplete Phrack/2600 search, failed Usenet search) aren't resolvable through more reasoning — they require either manual archive work or better search tooling than was available this pass, and are correctly flagged as open research gaps rather than papered over with a confident claim. Point 3 (OED vs. NYT citation comparability) is a fair methodological caveat that doesn't change the report's bottom line, since both data points still anchor a *range*, not a single date — the point stands regardless of exactly how the two source types compare. Point 4 (grey-hat inference) is genuinely just inference, correctly labeled as such rather than overstated; it's presented as supporting reasoning, not as a citation-grade fact, which is the right confidence level.

**The range itself, stated plainly:** based on available evidence, "black hat hacker" and "white hat hacker" moved from *emerging* usage to *common, mainstream-recognized* usage somewhere across **roughly 1990 to 1999**, with the tightest, most confident sub-range being **the mid-to-late 1990s** — this is when the term was clearly established enough within the hacker/security subculture to be named into a professional conference (Black Hat, 1997) and clearly established enough to reach uncontroversial usage in a national newspaper with a general readership (NYT, 1999) and to spawn joke derivatives within that same subculture (grey hat, 1999). The earlier end of the range (1990) is the best-documented *floor*, not a confirmed origin point — given Popper's points 1–3, there's a real possibility (not confirmed, not refuted) that informal usage in BBS/phreaker circles predates 1990 by some further stretch, plausibly into the **mid-to-late 1980s**, given how the general Western-movie metaphor was already common enough in American English by the 1960s that hacker culture adopting it whenever it needed a good-guy/bad-guy shorthand wouldn't have required anything more than an obvious, low-effort borrowing. This earlier possibility is speculative pattern-matching, not a measured finding — it should be read as "plausible and unresolved," not as an extension of the confirmed 1990–1999 range.
