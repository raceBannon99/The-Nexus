# Did Neil deGrasse Tyson Say the "Lying Felon" Quote?

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/raceBannon99/The-Nexus/main/assets/first-principles-consulting-logo.png" align="right" width="220">

**Question:** Did Dr. Neil deGrasse Tyson say this: "How sad it must be, believing that scientists, historians, scholars, and journalists, would devote their entire lives to deceiving you. But a con man proven in court to be a lying felon is your beacon of truth and honesty."

## Synthesis (Agent Bradlee)

No — there is no evidence Neil deGrasse Tyson said this, and the evidence that does exist points toward misattribution. Three independent checks come back empty: the quote doesn't appear on Tyson's own official quotes archive (neildegrassetyson.com/quotes/), it doesn't appear on his Wikiquote page (which has its own separate, vetted "Misattributed" section — meaning editors there actively track false quotes attached to him, and this isn't one they've listed, positive or negative), and no dated interview, speech transcript, or verified tweet from Tyson carries this text.

What does exist is a traceable earlier version. Multiple independent sources trace the quote's lineage to a January 18, 2019 post by Twitter/X user @christophurious: "How sad it must be — believing that scientists, scholars, historians, economists, and journalists have devoted their entire lives to deceiving you, while a reality tv star with decades of fraud and exhaustively documented lying is your only beacon of truth and honesty." That's a meaningfully different sentence from the one in your question, and the difference is itself informative: "proven in court to be a lying felon" could only apply after Trump's May 2024 New York felony conviction, five years after this 2019 posting. A genuine, single-authored quote doesn't organically get discovered in an earlier form that couldn't have contained a fact from the future — that's the signature of a chain-quote (sometimes called a copypasta) that mutates as it recirculates, not a fixed utterance from one person on one occasion. At least three worded variants of this same core sentiment are documented in circulation, each anchored to different public figures.

This fits a well-established pattern specific to Tyson. He has a documented history — independently covered by Snopes, The Federalist, and others — of having fabricated quotes attached to his name: a fake George W. Bush anecdote about "the God who named the stars," a parody Twitter account's rant mistaken for his real words, and a viral prank-engineered fake quote that spread across Reddit, Facebook, and Pinterest. Misattribution to Tyson specifically is a recurring phenomenon, not a one-off possibility.

One honest gap, surfaced by Popper's stress test below and worth stating plainly: no major fact-checking organization (Snopes, PolitiFact, AP Fact Check) appears to have published a dedicated, formal fact-check specifically on this exact quote. The conclusion here rests on convergent independent research — his own archive, Wikiquote, absence of any primary-source hit, and the traceable earlier variant — not a single authoritative "Rated False" verdict you could point to. That's a meaningfully weaker evidentiary position than a formal fact-check, but it's also exactly the standard fact-checkers themselves use to label a quote "unverified": the burden sits on producing the speaker's actual words, and nobody has.

**Bottom line: treat this as a misattributed/fabricated quote, not confirmed Tyson.** If you want to use the sentiment, attribute it to "widely circulated online, original source unclear" rather than to Tyson by name.

## What Must Be Fundamentally True? (Agent Euclid)

For Tyson to have actually said this, there would need to be a locatable primary utterance — a tweet, an interview clip, a speech excerpt — that either his own quote archive, a dedicated fact-check, or a basic web search would surface. None does.

The alternative explanation — that this is a floating internet chain-quote that picked up Tyson's name along the way, the way such quotes routinely pick up whichever prominent, articulate public figure best fits the sentiment — explains every observed fact without requiring anything unlocated:

- It explains why the wording mutates across instances (chain quotes have no canonical fixed text to check against).
- It explains why an earlier-dated version exists that couldn't contain a later fact ("proven in court" post-dating the 2019 posting by five years) — a real utterance doesn't get discovered in a form that predates events it describes; a meme does, because each retelling updates it to match the current news cycle.
- It explains the total absence from Tyson's own archive and Wikiquote's actively-maintained page.
- It fits a documented pattern: this is not the first time a fabricated quote has been hung on Tyson specifically.

Occam's razor favors misattribution. The "he really said it" hypothesis requires positing an utterance nobody has located despite years of circulation and public scrutiny of Tyson; the "misattributed meme" hypothesis requires no unexplained facts at all — everything observed is exactly what a floating, mutating meme looks like.

## How Could We Be Wrong? (Agent Popper)

Three real objections, not softened ones:

1. **Absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence.** Search engines don't index everything — a deleted tweet, an unrecorded talk-show aside, or a podcast appearance could contain a genuine remark in this spirit that simply hasn't been indexed or transcribed anywhere searchable.
2. **The 2019 @christophurious post may not be the true origin, only the earliest *found* instance.** It's possible that post itself was paraphrasing or quoting something said earlier — by Tyson or someone else — that hasn't surfaced in this search. "Earliest found" and "true origin" aren't the same claim.
3. **No formal fact-check organization has adjudicated this exact quote.** The conclusion here is built from independent research and reasoning, not a citable Snopes/PolitiFact/AP verdict. That's a real evidentiary gap, and it should be named rather than glossed over — this analysis could be wrong in a way a dedicated fact-check with direct access to Tyson or his representatives might resolve differently.

## What Is Likely True, and What Happens Next? (Agent Seldon)

Taking Popper's three objections in order:

**On absence of evidence:** true as a logical point, but the practical standard fact-checkers use is exactly the one applied here — the burden sits on producing the speaker's actual words, not on disproving a negative. Three independent checks (Tyson's own curated archive, Wikiquote's actively-maintained misattribution-tracking page, and no primary-source hit anywhere in general search) all coming back empty is the standard bar for a "Not Verified" or "False" rating, even though it can't be airtight proof of a negative. This doesn't change the practical conclusion Rick should act on.

**On the 2019 post not being the true origin:** possible in principle, but the specific framing — "reality tv star," "decades of fraud," commentary keyed to ongoing 2019 news about Trump's business and personal conduct — reads as generic 2019-era political commentary, not as Tyson's typical register. Tyson's own well-documented public commentary skews toward science literacy, space policy, and NASA funding, not this style of partisan political rhetoric. Even if the chain runs deeper than @christophurious, it's far more likely to terminate in general political commentary than specifically in Tyson.

**On the missing formal fact-check:** correct, and worth stating without overclaiming — this should be presented to Rick as "well-evidenced misattribution from independent research," not "officially debunked by Snopes." A dedicated fact-check may simply not exist yet because, despite wide circulation, this specific quote hasn't crossed whatever threshold triggers a formal fact-check assignment at those organizations.

**Forecast:** the chance that a genuine primary-source Tyson utterance matching this text (or a clear precursor to it) surfaces in the future is low — the range runs from roughly 2% to 10%, with a median around 5%. The low end reflects that this meme has circulated for over six years without anyone producing a receipt, despite Tyson being one of the most publicly quote-checked scientists alive (precisely because of his prior misattribution history, people who follow him tend to check his quotes). The higher end of the range accounts for the real possibility that some obscure, unindexed remark in a similar spirit exists and inspired the meme's phrasing, even if the exact wording is not his.
