# iSeatz External Exposure — A Pre-Onboarding Briefing for an Incoming vCISO

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/raceBannon99/The-Nexus/main/assets/first-principles-consulting-logo.png" align="right" width="220">

**Question:** Rick's friend Steve Winterfeld is taking a job as vCISO of iSeatz. Steve reframed his ask directly, and this report answers that exact framing: *"Without knowing the specifics of your platform, here is the bottom line: What are iSeatz's highest-risk externally visible assets, vulnerabilities, and misconfigurations? How confident are you that those assets actually belong to iSeatz? Specifically, I want to understand your capabilities in three areas: (1) Data and credential exposure — have you identified employee credentials, corporate email accounts, infostealer logs, leaked company information, criminal discussions involving iSeatz, or access to iSeatz systems being offered for sale? (2) Internet-facing attack surface — what iSeatz domains, subdomains, certificates, IP addresses, cloud services, VPNs, portals, APIs, and potentially vulnerable services are publicly accessible? (3) External security posture — what can you determine about patching, configuration, exposed technologies, security headers, reputation, misconfigurations, and likely vulnerabilities? In short, what capabilities does your platform provide that are comparable to — or extend beyond — tools and services such as Shodan, Have I Been Pwned, and SecurityScorecard?"*

## Bradlee — So, what's the answer?

**Bottom line, ranked by risk.** (1) A pair of VPN hostnames (`vpn-v2.lxp.iseatz.com`, `vpn-dev-v2.lxp.iseatz.com`) whose existence was permanently disclosed via public certificate logs, even though neither resolves publicly today — VPN gateways are the single most common initial-access vector in real-world ransomware intrusions, so confirm what VPN infrastructure actually exists and its patch status; don't read "doesn't resolve now" as "doesn't exist." (2) Heavy per-client environment sprawl on iSeatz's "LXP" platform — separate dev/qa/staging/sandbox/performance-test/production copies for named enterprise clients American Express, IHG, and Qantas — because non-production environments are disproportionately where forgotten, under-patched infrastructure survives, and a security incident touching an Amex- or IHG-branded environment creates fallout disproportionate to iSeatz's own size. (3) `itin-sync`, a live production *and* live staging API on AWS API Gateway — a genuinely reachable, addressable surface today, not a historical artifact. (4) Inconsistent security-header hardening across properties — the marketing site has minimal protection while an internal tool has excellent protection, pointing to a security-ownership gap rather than a skills gap. **None of the above is a confirmed exploitable vulnerability** — this report did not and could not run exploitation, active scanning, or credentialed testing (see the capabilities section below). These are the four places Steve's team should point real, authorized testing first.

**Confidence these assets actually belong to iSeatz.** High for every subdomain listed below — they all resolve under `iseatz.com`'s own DNS zone, and controlling the DNS record is the actual attribution signal, regardless of whether the backing server sits on shared AWS/Cloudflare infrastructure (it does, for nearly everything found). Medium for business-relationship *inferences* drawn from naming — e.g., `amex-e2-soap` strongly implies an Amex-specific integration, but naming is a strong signal, not documentary proof of exact scope. The full list (~65 names) is a **floor, not a ceiling**: several certificates found are wildcards, which by design hide an unknown number of additional live hostnames from any outside-in search.

**1) Data and credential exposure.** No — with a caveat that matters more than the "no." iSeatz does not appear as a named breach *source* in HaveIBeenPwned's public list of 1,027 breaches, and no public news reporting of an iSeatz-specific incident, leak, or criminal-forum listing turned up in web search. **But this engagement had no access to the checks that would actually answer Steve's question** — a domain-verified HaveIBeenPwned search (shows every breach containing an `@iseatz.com` address; requires proving domain ownership), any dark-web or criminal-marketplace monitoring feed, or any infostealer-log aggregator. "Nothing found in what we could check" is a coverage gap, not a clearance. This is the single highest-priority real gap in this report, and the recommended fix is concrete: run domain-verified HIBP the day Steve has standing to prove ownership, and budget for a dark-web/stealer-log monitoring subscription (Flare, Recorded Future, SpyCloud, Constella, or similar) — genuinely outside what a one-time passive-OSINT pass can do.

**2) Internet-facing attack surface.** What's publicly accessible, found via DNS, certificate-transparency logs, and HTTP checks only (no port scanning):
- **Domains/subdomains:** ~65 confirmed live or cert-issued hostnames under `iseatz.com`, dominated by the `lxp.iseatz.com` platform's per-client environment sprawl (Amex/IHG/Qantas × dev/qa/stg/sandbox/pt/prod) — full inventory in the table below.
- **Certificates:** all Google Trust Services-issued (via Cloudflare), short-lived and auto-renewing where checked — no expired or self-signed certs found in the sample checked, but only a few were individually inspected.
- **IP addresses:** root domain behind an AWS ELB; most live subdomains sit behind Cloudflare or CloudFront (shared, non-attributable IPs by design); one exception — `test-cognito.sb.lxp.iseatz.com` resolves to a bare AWS IP with no CDN/WAF in front of it, unlike everything else found.
- **Cloud services:** AWS (Route53 DNS, API Gateway, CloudFront, ELB — confirmed via CloudHealth domain-verification record too), Cloudflare (edge/CDN), Google Workspace (email), HubSpot (marketing-site CMS).
- **VPNs:** two hostnames with issued certs (above), not currently publicly resolving — status unconfirmed, not cleared.
- **Portals/APIs:** `itin-sync` (itinerary-sync API, live prod + staging, AWS API Gateway), `amex-e2-soap` (legacy SOAP integration), `amex-mobile`, `itrackz` (live), `onetimesecret` (a live, internet-reachable internal secrets-sharing tool), `regression-tests-reports` (live CloudFront distribution, 403 at root — access restricted but not confirmed safe beyond that one path).
- **Potentially vulnerable services:** none *confirmed* vulnerable — this report identifies where to look, not confirmed weaknesses. The legacy-SOAP integration, the unusually-unprotected Cognito sandbox, and the unconfirmed VPN infrastructure are the three places most likely to reward a closer, authorized look first.

**3) External security posture.** Patching/version-level detail is not visible from passive recon (that requires active scanning this engagement didn't run) — what can be said: certificates checked were current and auto-renewing (a good hygiene signal), DNSSEC is unsigned on the root zone (common, low-severity), and security-header configuration is inconsistent across properties — strong on an internal tool, minimal (no `X-Frame-Options`, bare-bones CSP) on the public marketing site. Reputation: no adverse public reporting found (breach coverage, security-press mentions) — again, a coverage-limited "nothing found," not a clean bill. The clearest posture signal overall is **inconsistency**: the organization demonstrably knows how to configure security correctly somewhere, it just isn't applied uniformly — that's a governance/ownership finding, not a technical-skill finding, and it's usually the faster one to fix.

**Platform capabilities vs. Shodan / HaveIBeenPwned / SecurityScorecard.** Direct comparison, capability by capability:
- **Shodan** does continuous, internet-wide *active* scanning — open ports, service banners, software/version fingerprints. This report did **none of that** (no active scanning, by deliberate ethical choice — see Popper's section). What this report **does exceed Shodan on**: certificate-transparency-based subdomain discovery surfaces an organization's *named, purpose-labeled* hosts (e.g., which subdomain belongs to which named client integration) more directly than Shodan's IP/port-centric indexing does. What Shodan would add on top of this report: actual service/version fingerprinting on whatever's genuinely listening, which is exactly the next authorized step to take.
- **HaveIBeenPwned** has two tiers: a public "is Company X itself a breach source" list (this report checked that directly — no match) and a domain-verified "which specific employee emails have leaked" search (this report **cannot** do that — it requires proof of domain ownership Steve doesn't have yet). So: this report matches HIBP's free tier exactly, and explicitly cannot match its verified tier.
- **SecurityScorecard/BitSight**-class platforms combine active scanning, a proprietary sensor network (botnet/malware C2 sinkhole data, compromised-host detection), continuous monitoring with alerting, and a peer-benchmarked letter-grade score — none of which this one-time passive pass can replicate. What this report provides that a scored platform typically doesn't: full transparency into *why* — every finding above traces to a specific, independently-checkable DNS record, certificate, or HTTP header, not a black-box score. That's a genuine, different kind of value (verifiable reasoning over a summary grade), but it's not a substitute for continuous, active, benchmarked monitoring — Steve should still budget for one of these platforms alongside, not instead of, engagements like this one.

## Sherlock — What are the facts?

**Company identity and business context.** iSeatz (`iseatz.com`) is a New Orleans-based travel and loyalty commerce technology provider, founded 1999 (domain registered January 2000). In 2024 it launched "LXP," a newer "Travel-as-a-Service" platform. Publicly named clients include American Express, IHG Hotels & Resorts, and Wyndham Hotels & Resorts; passive technical recon (below) independently corroborates Amex and IHG, and additionally surfaces Qantas as a named integration partner not mentioned in the marketing coverage found. This matters for risk framing: iSeatz is not just defending its own data, it's a potential pivot point into materially larger, more valuable enterprise clients — a supply-chain angle Euclid expands on below.

**DNS, registrar, and email.** `iseatz.com` is registered through Network Solutions (created 2000-01-24, last updated 2023-01-18); DNS is hosted on AWS Route53; **DNSSEC is unsigned** (a common baseline, not a severe finding — see Popper). Mail is routed through Google Workspace (MX records). The SPF record (`v=spf1 ip4:52.0.200.203 include:amextravel.com include:4857126.spf03.hubspotemail.net include:_spf.google.com ~all`) authorizes a specific static AWS-hosted sending IP, HubSpot's marketing-email infrastructure, Google Workspace, and — notably — `amextravel.com`, independently corroborating the American Express relationship. DNS TXT records also carry domain-verification strings for a wide set of third-party SaaS platforms: Adobe, Apple, Atlassian, Cisco (Webex), CloudHealth (AWS cost management, confirming AWS as their primary cloud), Figma, HubSpot/Mandrill, OpenAI, and Anthropic — each one is a place an employee's single-sign-on credentials could plausibly be phished or reused, i.e., part of the real human-layer attack surface even though none of this indicates a vulnerability by itself.

**Web edge and hosting stack.** `iseatz.com` (root, no `www`) sits behind an AWS Elastic Load Balancer and issues a 301 redirect to `www.iseatz.com`. `www.iseatz.com` is fronted by Cloudflare and backed by HubSpot's CMS (confirmed via `x-hs-portal-id: 4857126`, matching the portal ID in the SPF record above) — the public marketing site is not self-hosted infrastructure. Its security headers are minimal: `content-security-policy: upgrade-insecure-requests` only (no real policy), `strict-transport-security` present, `x-content-type-options: nosniff` present, but **no `x-frame-options` or `frame-ancestors` directive** — a clickjacking-protection gap, low severity on a marketing site but worth noting as a hardening gap. By contrast, `onetimesecret.iseatz.com` (a self-hosted secrets-sharing tool, live, fronted by Cloudflare) returns a comprehensive, well-configured header set: full CSP with `object-src 'none'` and `frame-ancestors 'self'`, `x-frame-options: SAMEORIGIN`, `cross-origin-opener-policy`, `cross-origin-resource-policy`, HSTS with `includeSubDomains`. The contrast between these two properties is itself a finding: the organization clearly knows how to configure headers well, just not consistently across every public-facing property.

**Certificate-transparency subdomain enumeration.** Using the public certificate-transparency API (`certspotter.com`; `crt.sh` was tried first and repeatedly timed out under load, a known reliability issue with that service), 100 issued-certificate records for `iseatz.com` and subdomains yielded **65 unique hostnames** — summarized in the table below and the diagram in Tufte's section. Key patterns:
- A heavy per-client environment structure under `lxp.iseatz.com`: `dev`, `qa`, `stg`, `sandbox`, `pt` (performance test), and production variants, separately for Amex, IHG, and Qantas (e.g., `amex-en-ca-lxp-{dev,int,qa,stg,prod}.ui.iseatz.com`, `ihg-hertz-earn-transfer-{us-east-1,us-west-2}.prod.lxp.iseatz.com`, `qantas-au-lxp-{qa,stg}.ui.iseatz.com`).
- Named integration endpoints: `amex-e2-soap.iseatz.com` (a legacy SOAP-protocol API — SOAP tooling tends to lag behind REST/GraphQL in active maintenance industry-wide), `amex-mobile.iseatz.com`, `amex-travel-e2/e3.iseatz.com`, `itin-sync.iseatz.com` (itinerary sync — confirmed **live in both production and staging**, resolving to AWS API Gateway custom domains), `itrackz.iseatz.com` (live, Cloudflare-fronted).
- Two VPN hostnames, `vpn-v2.lxp.iseatz.com` and `vpn-dev-v2.lxp.iseatz.com`, each with an issued public certificate but **neither currently resolves via public DNS A record** — the certificate-transparency log entry is permanent regardless (CA/Browser Forum rules require all publicly-trusted certificate issuance to be logged, forever), so the existence and naming convention of VPN infrastructure is now public knowledge whether or not the endpoint stays reachable.
- `onetimesecret.iseatz.com` and `regression-tests-reports.iseatz.com` are both live and internet-reachable. The latter (an AWS CloudFront distribution, presumably for CI/QA test-report artifacts) returns **HTTP 403 at the root** — access appears restricted rather than openly serving content, but this was checked only at the root path; see Popper's caveat below.
- `test-cognito.sb.lxp.iseatz.com` resolves directly to a bare AWS IP (`18.160.41.33`) with **no CDN/WAF fronting it**, unlike every other live property checked, which sit behind Cloudflare or CloudFront.
- Several certificates are wildcards (`*.lxp.iseatz.com`, `*.dev.lxp.iseatz.com`, `*.qa.lxp.iseatz.com`, `*.sandbox.lxp.iseatz.com`, `*.stg.lxp.iseatz.com`, `*.amex-cert.lxp.iseatz.com`, `*.amex-prod.lxp.iseatz.com`, `*.pt.lxp.iseatz.com`), meaning the true live-hostname count is understated by the 65 explicit names found.

**TLS certificate spot-check.** Certificates checked on `www.iseatz.com` and `amex-prod-dark.iseatz.com` were both current, non-expired, and issued by Google Trust Services (short validity windows consistent with automated renewal, likely via Cloudflare's managed TLS) — no expired or self-signed certificates found in the sample checked.

**Public code exposure.** A public GitHub repository search for "iseatz" returned 7 matching repositories, including several personal accounts using a company-affiliation naming pattern (an `-iseatz` suffix on the username) — a pattern that reveals employee names discoverable via GitHub, which this report deliberately does not list (see Clarifying Questions). None of these repositories were opened or scanned for secrets — that crosses from passive inventory into active investigation of individually identifiable people's code and was left as a recommended next step for iSeatz's own team (e.g., a `gitleaks`/`trufflehog`-style scan run internally), not something to do inside this engagement.

**Breach and credential exposure.** iSeatz does not appear as a named breach source in HaveIBeenPwned's full public breach list (1,027 breaches, queried directly, no auth required). Web searches for "iSeatz breach," "iSeatz hacked," "iseatz.com" combined with "leaked"/"dark web"/"credentials," and "iSeatz vulnerability CVE" returned no relevant results — no public reporting of an iSeatz-specific security incident was found (the CVE search only surfaced an unrelated Cisco product with a similar name). As stated in Bradlee's synthesis, this is a coverage gap, not a clean bill of health — see What Couldn't Be Checked below.

**What could not be checked, and why.** No dark-web marketplace, criminal forum, or Tor-hosted source was accessed — this engagement has no access to that infrastructure and would not use it even if available, since that crosses from passive OSINT into territory requiring dedicated, vetted commercial tooling. No Shodan account (would show open ports, service banners, and known-vulnerable-version fingerprints — active internet-wide scanning data this engagement cannot replicate passively). No HaveIBeenPwned domain-verified search (would show every breach containing an `@iseatz.com` address — requires proving domain ownership). No SecurityScorecard/BitSight-class subscription (would provide a scored, benchmarked rating synthesizing signals like these against peer companies). No active vulnerability scanning or port scanning was performed against any live host — everything above is either DNS/certificate metadata published by design or a root-path HTTP header check, equivalent to what a browser does visiting the bare URL.

## Euclid — What must be fundamentally true?

Three first-principles points underlie everything above:

**A company's real internet-facing attack surface is always larger than any list an outsider (or often the company itself) can produce, because certificate and DNS record creation is decentralized.** Any engineer on any team can spin up a subdomain and get a publicly-trusted certificate issued for it in minutes, with no central security review gate required by default. Certificate-transparency logging is a permanent, public, regulatorily-mandated side effect of getting a certificate at all — meaning the *existence* of an asset leaks the moment a cert is issued, independent of whether that asset is ever meant to be internet-facing, stays live, or gets decommissioned later. This is why the VPN-hostname finding matters even though neither hostname currently resolves: the leak already happened, permanently, regardless of iSeatz's current intent.

**Asset-attribution confidence is a gradient, not a binary, and the gradient tracks who controls what record — not what infrastructure sits behind it.** A hostname resolving under `iseatz.com`'s own DNS zone is high-confidence theirs because DNS-zone control is the actual chain of custody, regardless of whether the backing IP is a shared AWS or Cloudflare address serving thousands of unrelated tenants (it usually is, for any modern cloud-hosted company). This is the correct frame for answering Steve's explicit attribution-confidence question: don't confuse "shared cloud IP" with "uncertain ownership" — those are different axes entirely.

**A B2B SaaS platform serving branded enterprise clients has a bifurcated risk profile, and the second half is usually the more consequential one.** Risk to iSeatz's own data and operations is real, but risk of iSeatz being the weaker link that a sophisticated actor uses to reach American Express, IHG, or Qantas — companies with far larger blast radii, far more valuable data, and far more press attention on any incident — is the risk that actually drives prioritization for a vCISO in this specific seat. The heavy per-client environment sprawl (dev/qa/stg/sandbox/prod × 3+ named clients) found in certificate-transparency data isn't just an inventory-hygiene observation; it's evidence of exactly the kind of surface where that pivot risk concentrates.

## Popper — How could we be wrong?

Four objections, pressed directly rather than softened:

**1. "65 subdomains found" reads as more complete than it is.** Several certificates covering the LXP platform are wildcards, which by design don't require (and often don't get) individual log entries for every hostname they secure. The real number of live environments under `lxp.iseatz.com` alone is almost certainly larger than what passive certificate-transparency search can surface — this report's inventory is a documented floor, not anything close to exhaustive, and presenting it otherwise would overstate confidence Steve shouldn't have.

**2. The `regression-tests-reports.iseatz.com` finding (live, 403 at root) reads more reassuring than it should.** A 403 at the CloudFront/S3 root does not prove the whole distribution is inaccessible — misconfigured S3-backed CloudFront distributions commonly block bucket listing at the root while specific, guessable, or previously-shared object paths remain fully public. This wasn't tested further because doing so would mean path enumeration — active probing this engagement deliberately stayed out of — so "inconclusive, needs authorized follow-up" is the honest read, not "secure."

**3. "No breach found" and "no news coverage found" are being asked to do too much work if read as reassurance.** This report checked HIBP's list of *named breach sources* and general web search — neither is remotely equivalent to a domain-verified HIBP employee-credential search, a paid dark-web/stealer-log monitoring feed, or actual criminal-forum access, none of which were available here. A true "clean" finding requires those tools; what this report actually has is "no finding from a narrow slice of what's checkable," which is a meaningfully weaker claim and needs to stay clearly labeled as such throughout, not softened in the retelling.

**4. DNSSEC-unsigned is being listed as a finding, but the base rate needs to temper how it's weighted.** Most domains, including the large majority of major enterprises, don't sign DNSSEC — it's a real, if minor, gap (unsigned zones are more susceptible to DNS cache-poisoning/spoofing attacks), but calling it out with the same visual weight as the VPN or per-client-sprawl findings would misrepresent its relative priority for Steve's first 90 days.

## Seldon — What is likely to happen next?

Each of Popper's four points is resolved directly, not just logged, and the report's language above already reflects the resolution — restated here for traceability: **(1)** the 65-subdomain inventory is now explicitly labeled a floor throughout (Sherlock's section, the diagram, and this synthesis), not a complete count. **(2)** the `regression-tests-reports` finding was reframed from an implied positive ("blocked, so probably fine") to an explicit "inconclusive, don't assume safe" — this is reflected in Sherlock's writeup above rather than left as Popper raised it. **(3)** every mention of the breach/credential-exposure check throughout this report is qualified as a narrow, incomplete slice of the real question, with the domain-verified-HIBP and dark-web-monitoring gaps stated plainly rather than implied as covered. **(4)** DNSSEC is kept out of the top-four risk ranking in Bradlee's synthesis specifically because of its near-universal base rate, addressing Popper's weighting concern directly.

**Forward-looking, in range-with-median form as convention requires:**

- *How long would it take a moderately resourced attacker (or a red team) to reach the same subdomain inventory found here, using only free tools?* This isn't really a forecast — it's closer to present reality — but framed as a range for completeness: from a few minutes to a couple of hours, with a median well under an hour, since certificate-transparency search and DNS enumeration are exactly the first, lowest-effort steps any opportunistic attacker or professional red-teamer runs. The takeaway isn't "this report created new risk" (it didn't — everything here was already free and equally reachable by anyone) — it's that whatever's genuinely exploitable in this surface should be assumed to already be discoverable by anyone who'd bother to look, which argues for urgency rather than security-through-obscurity.
- *What share of the discovered (and undiscovered) non-production environments likely carry at least one meaningfully overdue patch or a forgotten-but-still-live service, based on general base rates for organizations with this degree of per-client environment sprawl?* This is reasoned judgment from general industry pattern, explicitly **not** a measured claim about iSeatz specifically, since nothing in this engagement tested any environment's actual patch level. The range runs from roughly a quarter to well over half of such environments at comparably-sized organizations, with a median guess around 40–50%. The size of the range reflects genuine uncertainty about iSeatz's specific engineering discipline, which only Steve's own team can resolve.
- *Once Steve's team runs a proper authorized active scan (Shodan-class tooling, or a commercial vulnerability scanner) against this expanded surface, how long until they find at least one finding requiring remediation?* Median guess is within the first one to two weeks of doing it properly, given the scale of environment sprawl found here — could be immediate (day one), could take longer if actual hygiene turns out better than the base rates above suggest. Framed as a range because it depends entirely on facts this engagement has no way to observe from outside.

## Turing — Should any of this become a skill?

Yes — built during this run, not just proposed. The methodology developed here (DNS/WHOIS via `dig`/`whois`, certificate-transparency subdomain enumeration via `certspotter.com` since `crt.sh` proved unreliable under load, HTTP security-header inspection via `curl`, TLS certificate spot-checks via `openssl`, HaveIBeenPwned's public breach-corpus check, and public GitHub repository search) is entirely reusable for any future Nexus engagement asking "what does Company X's external footprint look like" — a recurring category of question (pre-acquisition diligence, a new security hire's onboarding, general curiosity about a vendor or partner). Documented as a new skill, `nexus-external-recon`, at `.claude/skills/nexus-external-recon/SKILL.md` in the vault — it captures the technique, the passive-only ethical boundary, the specific reliability workaround (certspotter over crt.sh), and the standing reminder to raise the public-repo publication question as a Bradlee clarifying question before running, the way this engagement did.
