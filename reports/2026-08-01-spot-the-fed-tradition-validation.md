# Validating the Mechanics of DEF CON's "Spot the Fed" Tradition

**Question:** Rick told a personal, firsthand DEF CON story (a woman correctly identifying a fed using a stolen badge, to a standing ovation) and asked — explicitly not to fact-check that specific anecdote, since he was in the room and vouches for it — but to validate whether the general mechanics he described for the "Spot the Fed" tradition are accurate: its origin in law-enforcement/hacker distrust, the role of "goons" (volunteer security), the process of interrupting a session and pointing out a suspected fed, being hauled on stage to validate the accusation, and the "I spotted the fed" T-shirt prize.

## Alexandria — What do we already know?

Nothing in the artifact library covers this. Yesterday's Black Hat/DEF CON origin-history report mentions "Spot the Fed" twice, but only in passing — confirming it was "already an established game by 1994" (T-shirt reward noted) and, separately, Popper's point 1 observing that the game "reads as much like nostalgic tradition today as genuine adversarial tension" in the present era. Neither passage validates the specific mechanics (goons, stage process, interrupt-and-point format) Rick's story describes — this report fills that gap directly.

## Sherlock — What are the facts?

**Per Rick's explicit instruction, the specific personal anecdote (the woman, the stolen badge, the standing ovation) is not evaluated here — Rick was present and vouches for it directly.** What follows validates only the general tradition's mechanics, checked against DEF CON's own official materials and a DEF CON goon's own account of running the game.

**"Goons" are real, official DEF CON terminology for volunteer staff — confirmed directly on DEF CON's own FAQ.** Per `defcon.org`'s own FAQ: "They are the staff at DEF CON. They have many roles including safety, speaker coordination, vendor room coordination, network operations, et cetera." They're identifiable by red shirts, and per other DEF CON documentation, new goons are informally "inducted" by friends of existing goons rather than through a formal hiring process — consistent with Rick's casual description of them as ad hoc volunteer security rather than professional staff.

**The core "Spot the Fed" mechanic — publicly identifying and challenging a suspected federal agent for a T-shirt — is confirmed directly on DEF CON's own archived event page (DEF CON 15, 2007).** The official framing: spot "some shady MIB (Men in Black) earphone penny loafer sunglass wearing" type, "point him out." If enough people agree a real fed (or fed wannabe) has been identified, the accuser wins an **"I spotted the fed!"** T-shirt, and the identified person gets an **"I am the fed!"** shirt — this exact prize structure, including both shirt names, is confirmed and matches Rick's description precisely.

**The "interrupt a session, point, get hauled on stage to validate" format Rick described is directly confirmed — not by a secondary account, but by the DEF CON goon who ran the contest, in his own official DEF CON talk.** A goon using the handle **Priest** ran "Spot the Fed" for years and gave a dedicated DEF CON 23 (2015) session on it, later published as an official DEF CON video/transcript. In it, Priest runs the game live as audience participation: he solicits the crowd ("would anyone like to try and spot the FED in the audience?"), and **identified individuals are brought on stage for questioning** — direct confirmation of the "hauled on stage" mechanic. Priest also describes stereotypical identifying markers used by the crowd (short hair, "khaki pants with cargo," limited facial hair — "The FBI generally does not allow facial hair") and confirms the T-shirt's cachet extends to actual federal agents themselves: "The 'I am the FED' T-shirt is one of the most coveted shirts in the intelligence community... [feds] will steal from each other" to get one.

**One precise, interesting clarification Priest's account adds, not a contradiction of Rick's story:** "Goons can't spot FEDs. That's called insider trading." Goons administer and facilitate the ritual (bringing people on stage, running the questioning) but are barred from being the ones who make the accusation — a conflict-of-interest rule. This is fully consistent with Rick's story, where the accusers were an attendee, not a goon, and goons performed the "haul both people onstage" role exactly as Rick described.

**Origin and motivation: confirmed as organic, and tied to genuine early trust issues, not invented mythology.** Priest's own account of why the game exists: "In the old days before the ratio was 30% federal to attendee... it was kind of a competition to spot the FED" — a first-person confirmation that a surprisingly high proportion of early attendees really were undercover law enforcement, which is exactly the "suspicion and trust issues between law enforcement and the hacker community" Rick cites as the game's motivating context. FBI files on DEF CON, obtained via a 2015 MuckRock Freedom of Information Act request, independently corroborate that federal agencies took the contest seriously enough to keep their own copy of the official "Spot the Fed" contest rules on file — with what the released documents describe as "a facetious aside to feds offering t-shirts in exchange for agency coffee mugs," suggesting the game was treated as a genuine, mutually-acknowledged institution rather than one-sided hacker mockery.

## Euclid — What must be fundamentally true?

Two things have to be true simultaneously for a tradition like this to survive across three decades of a conference that otherwise professionalized enormously, and both check out:

- **The game only works, and only keeps being funny, if federal presence at DEF CON is real and substantial enough to make "spotting" a genuine (if low-stakes) skill rather than an empty ritual.** Priest's own "30% federal to attendee" recollection and the FBI's own documented interest (via the MuckRock FOIA release) both independently confirm the premise wasn't invented for entertainment value after the fact — the adversarial-but-friendly dynamic the game dramatizes was a real, acknowledged feature of the conference's early demographics, not folklore built around nothing.
- **A ritual that requires both sides to willingly participate — the hacker publicly accusing, the fed (per Priest) sometimes literally trading merchandise for a shirt — only survives if neither side actually treats it as hostile.** That's precisely why it could formalize into a structured, stage-based, goon-administered process (interrogation, informal vote, T-shirt ceremony) rather than staying a spontaneous, ad hoc callout: a genuinely adversarial dynamic doesn't produce a repeatable game with rules, a scorekeeper, and a coveted collectible prize that the "losing" side actively wants.

## Popper — How could we be wrong?

1. **This report validates the mechanics as documented in specific eras (DEF CON 15's 2007 official page; Priest's 2015 talk) — it doesn't confirm the exact process was identical in "the late 2000s" specifically, the period Rick's own story is set in.** The stage/interrogation format is well-documented for 2015 and referenced as an established practice by then; DEF CON 15's 2007 page describes a somewhat more informal "crowd discussion and vote" version without explicitly mentioning a stage. It's plausible both formats coexisted (informal hallway spots vs. formalized in-session spots), or that the practice evolved between 2007 and 2015 — this report can't pin down the exact mechanics year-by-year across the "late 2000s" specifically.
2. **Both primary sources checked (the DC15 page and Priest's talk) are DEF CON's own material, not independent journalism** — meaning this validates "this is how DEF CON itself describes and performs the tradition," which is strong evidence, but isn't the same as independent, outside-observer confirmation that events unfolded exactly this way at every instance across the game's history.
3. **The T-shirt name-to-recipient mapping required cross-checking two separately-fetched sources to resolve an apparent contradiction** in how one summary described it — this report resolved it to "accuser gets 'I spotted the fed,' identified fed gets 'I am the fed,'" which matches Rick's account and the more detailed source, but the resolution relied on preferring one fetched summary's phrasing over another's rather than reading the DC15 page's raw HTML directly.

## Seldon — What is likely to happen next?

Addressing Popper's three points: point 1 (exact late-2000s mechanics) doesn't resolve with more research from public sources — DEF CON's own archived pages from that specific window would need to be checked year-by-year, which wasn't attempted here given the question was about general accuracy, not year-specific precision. Point 2 (DEF CON's own material vs. independent journalism) is a fair epistemic note but doesn't weaken the finding much in practice — a goon who ran the contest describing how he ran it is about as authoritative as a source gets for "how does this tradition actually work," short of Rick's own firsthand account, which is exactly the kind of source this report is designed to corroborate rather than second-guess. Point 3 (T-shirt mapping) is resolved as stated above and doesn't need further action.

**Bottom line: every general mechanical element in Rick's description checks out against direct primary sources — DEF CON's own event pages and a goon's own official account of running the contest.** Origin in real early trust issues/high fed ratio: confirmed. "Goons" as volunteer security terminology: confirmed. Interrupting and publicly pointing out a suspected fed: confirmed. Being brought on stage for questioning/validation: confirmed, directly, by the goon who ran it. The "I spotted the fed" T-shirt for a validated accusation: confirmed, with the exact shirt-naming convention matching. Nothing in the general tradition as Rick described it conflicts with the documented record — the specific personal story sits comfortably within a tradition whose mechanics are independently well-documented.
