# Women's Attendance at Black Hat, DEF CON, and BSides Las Vegas: What Can Actually Be Confirmed?

**Question:** Can hard numbers be found for how many women have attended Black Hat, DEF CON, and BSides Las Vegas over the years? Can the common claim that women's attendance has been rising be vouched for?

## Alexandria — What do we already know?

Nothing in the artifact library covers this. Yesterday's Spot the Fed report quoted Rick's own personal recollection — "you traditionally don't see a lot of women at DEF CON (although the numbers have improved over the years)" — as part of a story that report explicitly declined to fact-check per Rick's instruction. This report picks up that specific claim (numbers improving over the years) and tries to verify it directly, on its own terms, using real data rather than Rick's recollection. Yesterday's origin-history report also separately notes Black Hat's 2022 25th-anniversary shift toward "a formal Code of Conduct, scholarship programs, and diversity-focused programming" — institutional context worth carrying forward, but not itself an attendance number.

## Sherlock — What are the facts?

**The direct answer to the first question is no: none of the three conferences publish attendance-by-gender numbers, for any year.** This was checked directly, not just assumed:

- **DEF CON's own Transparency Report** (`defcon.org/html/links/dc-transparency.html`) — checked directly. Contains no attendee demographic data of any kind. The only gender-adjacent entry found anywhere on DEF CON's official pages was an enforcement statistic ("2 people banned for life due to harassing women," DEF CON 25) — a moderation-action count, not an attendance figure.
- **DEF CON's own FAQ** — also checked; no demographic breakdowns published.
- **Black Hat's own annual Attendee Survey PDFs** (2016, 2017 editions checked) — these are real, substantial documents (professional role, certifications, security concerns, budget priorities) but a direct fetch found no extractable gender-breakdown data in the accessible portions.
- **BSides Las Vegas** — no official attendance figures of any kind (gender or otherwise) were found published anywhere; general web search returned only logistics pages (dates, venue, general "2,500+ attendees" figures) with zero demographic detail.

This absence is itself informative and consistent with something structural, not sloppy record-keeping: **none of these three events register attendees by gender at all.** DEF CON is famously cash-only, no-registration-required for general admission specifically to preserve attendee anonymity (documented in yesterday's origin-history report's account of the 1993 founding and the cash/no-pre-registration norm Moss described directly). A conference culture built around not knowing who you are structurally cannot produce a gender headcount — the absence of data isn't an oversight, it follows directly from the events' own privacy norms.

**What does exist instead is a mix of qualitative accounts and narrow program-specific numbers — real, but not what was asked for.** A 2019-era feature ("Def Con Has Always Been Straight, White, and Male, But It's Finally Changing," Vice) is representative of the genre: it quotes an attendee (Elena Elkina) who "noticed a marked increase in the number of female attendees at this year's conference compared with last year's" — a real, named, on-record observation, but a single person's year-over-year impression, not a count. Separately, **Women in Security and Privacy (WISP) sponsored 92 women to attend DEF CON in 2019** — a real, specific, sourced number, but it's a scholarship-program headcount, not total female attendance (DEF CON overall runs in the tens of thousands).

**Black Hat's institutional diversity effort is well-documented and expanded materially over time, even without an attendance count.** Black Hat has run partnerships with Women in Security and Privacy (WISP), the International Consortium of Minority Cybersecurity Professionals (ICMCP), and Queercon; hosted a dedicated Women's Panel (documented from at least 2016); provided complimentary Briefings passes to nearly 40 women via a partnership with the Executive Women's Forum; and runs a "Community Lounge" featuring sessions on women and underrepresented groups in the field. This is real, verifiable institutional investment that grew over the 2010s into the 2020s — but institutional effort and outcome (actual attendance share) are two different things, and only the effort side has hard evidence here.

**The best available quantitative trend is a genuine longitudinal data series — but it measures the cybersecurity workforce broadly, not conference attendance specifically.** The (ISC)² Cybersecurity Workforce Study is a real, recurring, large-sample industry survey. Its own historical figures for women's share of the cybersecurity workforce: **roughly 11% around 2013, essentially flat through 2017** (a Black Hat 2017 panel independently cited the same "plateaued at about 11%" figure for the broader industry, corroborating (ISC)²'s number from a second source), then a **jump to roughly 20–25% starting around 2021** and holding in that range in the most recent surveys (22% per (ISC)²'s March 2025 report). This is a real, well-corroborated, multi-source-confirmed increase — but it describes who works in cybersecurity as a profession, which is a different (and only partially overlapping) population from who walks through the door at a specific conference in Las Vegas in any given year.

## Euclid — What must be fundamentally true?

Two structural facts explain why this question resists a clean answer, and why that's not a research failure:

- **A statistic can only exist if someone has an institutional reason to collect it, and these three events have specific, deliberate reasons not to.** DEF CON's cash-only, no-pre-registration model exists precisely to avoid creating exactly the kind of attendee record a gender count would require — it's not that DEF CON forgot to ask, it's that asking would contradict the anonymity norm the origin-history report already established as foundational to the event's identity since 1993. Black Hat, being the more corporate, badge-registered sibling, plausibly *could* collect this internally, but choosing not to publish it (while publishing detailed survey data on professional roles, budgets, and threat priorities) suggests either the data isn't collected in a form suitable for release, or isn't a metric the organizers have chosen to make public — this report can't distinguish between those two explanations from outside.
- **"Attendance is rising" and "the industry's workforce is more gender-balanced than a decade ago" are two different claims that get conflated in casual conversation, including in how this question was likely originally framed.** The (ISC)² data genuinely supports the second claim with real rigor. It does not, by itself, support the first — a hacker convention's attendee mix isn't guaranteed to track the broader professional workforce's gender composition, since conference attendance is shaped by additional factors (marketing, culture, cost, safety perceptions, the specific reputation of DEF CON vs. Black Hat vs. a smaller community event like BSides) that a workforce survey doesn't capture at all.

## Popper — How could we be wrong?

1. **"No public data was found" is not the same as "no data exists."** Black Hat in particular runs sophisticated, professionally-produced attendee surveys with real budgets behind them — it's plausible they collect gender data internally for sponsors or organizers and simply don't publish it as a headline figure. This report's searches were public-web-only; a request made directly to Black Hat's or DEF CON's press/organizer contacts might surface internal figures never indexed by search engines.
2. **The (ISC)² workforce trend, while real, may not generalize to conference attendance in either direction.** It's equally plausible that hacker-conference attendee demographics lag the broader professionalized workforce (older, more entrenched cultural patterns at a 30-year-old event) or that they lead it (younger, more terminally-online population reflecting demographic shifts before they show up in a workforce survey of employed professionals). This report has no way to adjudicate which pattern actually holds without conference-specific data — which, per Sherlock, doesn't appear to exist publicly.
3. **The qualitative accounts found (Elena Elkina's observation, the general "diversity is improving" narrative common in security-industry journalism) could reflect selection bias in what gets published.** A journalist is more likely to write "diversity is improving, here's a hopeful anecdote" than "diversity is stagnant, nothing to report" — meaning the available qualitative record likely over-represents the positive-trend narrative relative to how a neutral, systematic survey would characterize the same reality.
4. **The 2019 WISP figure (92 sponsored women) has no comparison point.** Without knowing whether WISP sponsored a similar, larger, or smaller number in other years, this single data point can't itself demonstrate a trend — it's cited here as evidence a scholarship mechanism exists and has real scale, not as trend evidence.

## Seldon — What is likely to happen next?

Addressing Popper's four points: point 1 (undisclosed internal data) isn't resolvable through more public web research — it would require a direct outreach to Black Hat or DEF CON's organizers, which is outside this report's scope but worth flagging as the single most promising next step if Rick wants a firmer answer. Point 2 (workforce trend vs. conference attendance) is a genuine, unresolvable gap given available evidence — correctly flagged as a limitation rather than papered over with an assumption in either direction. Point 3 (publication selection bias in the qualitative record) doesn't get resolved by more searching of the same kind of source; it would need a genuinely systematic count (e.g., photographic/badge-scan analysis of a specific DEF CON year), which isn't something this report can produce. Point 4 (single WISP data point) is correctly caveated as illustrative, not trend evidence.

**Direct answer to Rick's second question, stated as a range rather than a yes/no:** the likelihood that women's attendance at these three specific conferences has genuinely risen over the past decade or so — as opposed to holding roughly flat while diversity *programming and visibility* increased, which would produce the same qualitative "it feels different now" impression without an actual attendance-share change — runs from about **60% to 85%, with a median around 75%.** The lower end reflects genuine uncertainty about whether a specific hacker-convention population tracks the broader professionalized workforce; the higher end reflects that the (ISC)² workforce trend is real, large, and multi-year, that it's implausible for institutional recruitment efforts (scholarships, women's panels, complimentary passes, code-of-conduct changes) to have zero effect on who actually shows up, and that the qualitative record — while subject to selection bias — is consistent rather than contradicted by any source found in this research. This is a judgment call built from adjacent, imperfect evidence, not a measured statistic about the conferences themselves — that distinction is the whole point of this report.

**So: Rick can reasonably continue saying "the numbers have improved" as an honest impression consistent with real institutional change and a strong industry-wide trend** — but should not present it as a documented conference-attendance statistic, because that specific number does not appear to exist publicly for any of the three events.

## Tufte — How do we make this clear?

A simple table separating what's *actually measured* from what's *plausible but unmeasured* does more work here than prose alone, since the core finding is precisely that distinction:
