# David Sanger Interview Prep: The Perfect Weapon, Eight Years On

**Question:** Rick is interviewing David Sanger (NYT national security correspondent, author of *The Perfect Weapon: War, Sabotage, and Fear in the Cyber Age*, 2018 Cyber Canon Hall of Fame inductee) for 20 minutes on Thursday, 2026-09-17, at the **Open Source Security Summit 2026** — a "Fireside chat with David Sanger: The geopolitics of cybersecurity," moderated by Rick. What should Rick ask him — covering the book, what Sanger has done since, the 2020 HBO documentary adaptation, what's changed in cyber conflict and great-power competition since the book came out, and (update, 2026-09-14) whether there's a genuine way to connect his work to open-source software security specifically, given the venue.

## The Answer

Yes — and it's the single best question in the interview, not a bolt-on. The 2024 XZ Utils backdoor is the concrete bridge: a multi-year operation that spent two years building trust inside an open-source maintainer community before planting a remote-code-execution backdoor in a compression library almost every Linux system depends on through OpenSSH. Security researchers who examined it called it the most sophisticated software supply-chain attack ever caught, with patience and tradecraft that reads like an intelligence service's — though no government has ever been formally named. That is the exact story Sanger has spent his career telling, aimed at a target he hasn't written about at length: the trust model open source runs on, rather than a government network.

Lead with the newest material generally, and let the book surface as color, not as its own segment — Sanger has answered book-retrospective questions in hundreds of interviews since 2018, and a 20-minute slot can't compete with those on that ground. Given the venue and the session's own billing as "the geopolitics of cybersecurity," two questions now carry almost all of the value in this slot, with two more close behind. First, the spine: "You called cyber 'the perfect weapon' in 2018. In 2026, is that title still accurate, or has it been overtaken — by ransomware as a business, by China prepositioning inside American infrastructure, or by something else?" Second, immediately behind it: "XZ Utils showed a patient, resourced operation going after open-source trust networks instead of a government target. Is the open-source supply chain now a nation-state attack surface in its own right — and does a volunteer maintainer community have any real way to defend against an adversary willing to spend two years earning trust to abuse it?" That question does something the rest of the interview can't: it puts Sanger's specific expertise directly in front of an audience that lives inside open-source security every day, rather than asking him to generalize about a domain most of them know better than he does in the particulars.

Two more carry real but secondary value: pressing his April 2026 remark that AI "scares the hell out of every government" but is "too irresistible" into something concrete — what does an AI-accelerated intrusion or influence operation actually look like, and has he seen one; and challenging the Cold War metaphor in *New Cold Wars* directly, since reviewers have already flagged it as a possible oversimplification of a rivalry that's as much about technological and economic entanglement as military standoff.

Everything else is optional color: what he got most wrong in the book; whether the HBO documentary changed his understanding once officials were on camera; a closer on what's still underrated. None of these need dedicated segments — they're follow-ups if an earlier answer runs short, first to cut if it doesn't.

The practical risk stays time management, not content. Sanger tends to illustrate present-day points with historical detail; opening with the spine question and following immediately with the open-source bridge protects the two questions this specific audience most needs answered, before older material or a long historical detour can eat the clock.

## Facts (Sherlock)

- **The Perfect Weapon: War, Sabotage, and Fear in the Cyber Age** (Crown, 2018) — Sanger's account of cyber weapons becoming a central tool of statecraft: Stuxnet/Olympic Games against Iran, the 2014 Sony Pictures hack (North Korea), WannaCry, Russian interference in the 2016 U.S. election, and Chinese cyber-espionage, framed around the absence of any real deterrence doctrine or rules of the road for state cyber conflict. Inducted into the Cyber Canon Hall of Fame in 2020.
- **HBO documentary adaptation**, also titled *The Perfect Weapon*, directed by John Maggio, produced by Words & Pictures/RadicalMedia, debuted October 16, 2020. Built on the book but added new on-camera interviews with military and intelligence officials and fresh reporting, landing amid the SolarWinds compromise, which was publicly disclosed two months later (December 2020).
- **Major cyber/great-power developments since the book's 2018 publication** that a "what's changed" segment would likely draw on: the SolarWinds supply-chain compromise (disclosed Dec. 2020, attributed to Russia's SVR); the Colonial Pipeline ransomware attack (May 2021, DarkSide); Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine (Feb. 2022) and its accompanying cyber and information-warfare campaigns; the maturing of ransomware into an industrialized criminal business model; and Volt Typhoon, the Chinese state-linked campaign disclosed in 2023–2024 found prepositioned inside U.S. critical infrastructure (power, water, communications) — a materially different objective (disruption-on-demand) than the espionage-driven intrusions that dominated the book.
- **New Cold Wars: China's Rise, Russia's Invasion, and America's Struggle to Defend the West** (Crown, 2024), Sanger's most recent book (reported with Mary K. Brooks), a *New York Times* bestseller. It widens the frame from cyber specifically to great-power rivalry generally, explicitly invoking the Cold War as an analogy for the current U.S.-China-Russia dynamic, while still treating cyber, AI, and surveillance as central tools of that contest — quoted as writing that "technology is now as much a weapon as any military force." Foreign Affairs' review and others note the book's strength is insider access but flag the Cold War framing itself as a point of legitimate critique, arguing it may oversimplify a rivalry that is as much about technological and economic entanglement as classic military standoff.
- **April 30, 2026** — Sanger spoke at Dartmouth (Haldeman Hall), addressing how *unregulated AI use by governments* will "speed up the hacking process" and "the disinformation process," making cyber and information warfare faster and more scalable. His framing of the underlying tension: "this technology scares the hell out of every government I know, and yet it's too irresistible for them not to go play with." He separately argued the U.S. needs to prioritize addressing "intensifying rivalries" with China and Russia — consistent with *New Cold Wars*' thesis, suggesting his current public messaging is a continuation of that book's argument rather than a new position.
- No corroborating detail was found on what Sanger has specifically reported or said about *this* interview's exact date window (September 2026) beyond the April 2026 Dartmouth remarks — that is the most recent on-record material located.
- **The Open Source Security Summit** is organized by Bitwarden, now in its seventh year (2026), a free virtual event billed as "a forum to explore the intersection of open source and security." Sanger is a 2026 headliner alongside Joseph Menn (*Cult of the Dead Cow*) and researcher Adrian Sanabria. His session is listed as "Fireside chat with David Sanger: The geopolitics of cybersecurity," moderated by Rick Howard of the Cyber Canon Project — confirming the interview's actual framing and audience.
- **The XZ Utils backdoor (CVE-2024-3094)**, discovered by chance on March 29, 2024, by developer Andres Freund while investigating unusual CPU usage in `sshd`. A GitHub contributor using the identity "Jia Tan" spent roughly two years (beginning June 2022, 450+ commits) building trust as a co-maintainer of the widely used `xz`/`liblzma` compression library before inserting a backdoor giving an attacker with a specific private key remote code execution over OpenSSH on affected Linux systems. It received a maximum CVSS score of 10.0. Security researchers widely described the patience, discipline, and operational tradecraft as consistent with a well-resourced intelligence operation — but **no government has been formally, publicly attributed** as the sponsor; this is suspected tradecraft, not confirmed state attribution.
- **Log4Shell / Log4j (CVE-2021-44228, disclosed December 2021)** — a critical, trivially exploitable vulnerability in a near-ubiquitous open-source Java logging library, distinct in kind from XZ Utils: not a deliberately planted backdoor but a systemic flaw in widely reused open-source code, exploited at mass scale by a range of actors including state-linked groups. Frequently cited alongside XZ Utils as bookending the open-source supply-chain risk conversation — one an accident of scale, the other a deliberate infiltration.
- **The Biden administration's March 2023 National Cybersecurity Strategy** proposed shifting liability toward software makers for insecure products, but explicitly carved out unpaid open-source maintainers and contributors from that liability shift, stating responsibility should not fall on "the open-source developer of a component that is integrated into a commercial product." CISA's parallel Secure by Design initiative (2023– ) treats this as a standing policy tension: incentivize vendors to secure their software while protecting the volunteer maintainers much of that software depends on.

## Adversary/Campaign Characterization (Ryan)

Applicable as of this update, specifically to the XZ Utils reference above, since attribution language appears in this report and must be tagged carefully rather than left loose. Per the standing campaign-vs-actor-attribution convention: **campaign attribution** (is this a single, coherent operation with consistent tradecraft) is comparatively strong here — a single persona, a sustained multi-year infiltration of one project's trust structure, and a single technical payload point to one coordinated operation, not opportunistic or crowd-sourced activity. **Actor attribution** (who specifically is behind it) is weak — no security vendor, government, or the open-source project itself has published a formal nation-state attribution; "resembles intelligence-service tradecraft" is an inference from operational patience and resourcing, not confirmed sponsorship. Any interview question referencing XZ Utils should be phrased to reflect that gap explicitly (as the questions below do) rather than asserting a named state actor. Volt Typhoon, by contrast, already carries a considerably more confident nation-state attribution to China in prior public reporting than XZ Utils does to any state — that comparison is noted for context but not re-litigated to a specific tier here, since this report didn't independently source Volt Typhoon's attribution evidence. No kill-chain/Diamond Model/ATT&CK full characterization is warranted for either — this remains interview prep, not an incident analysis — but the attribution-confidence distinction itself is load-bearing for how the questions below should be worded.

## First-Principles Analysis (Euclid)

The value of any interview question is a function of two things: how much genuinely new information it can extract, and how directly that information connects to something the audience already cares about. Applying that test here:

Book-retrospective questions ("what surprised you when you were reporting it," "what's the origin story") score low on novelty — Sanger has been asked these in nearly every interview and panel appearance since 2018, across the original book tour, the 2020 HBO press cycle, and years of speaking engagements since. Any answer he gives is likely to be a well-rehearsed version of something already on the public record.

Forward-looking questions anchored to his *own most recent, least-repeated statements* score highest on novelty. His April 2026 Dartmouth remarks on AI accelerating hacking and disinformation are the newest identifiable public position he's taken, and they haven't yet been pressed into a concrete, falsifiable claim ("what does this actually look like") in the sources located. Similarly, his 2024 *New Cold Wars* thesis — that cyber is now one instrument inside a broader great-power rivalry rather than a freestanding "perfect weapon" — is a genuine evolution from the 2018 book's framing, and asking him to reconcile the two titles directly (is cyber still *the* perfect weapon, or has it been subsumed into something bigger) forces a synthesis he likely hasn't been asked to state that plainly and concisely before.

The HBO documentary sits in between: it's six years old and heavily covered in its own 2020 press cycle, but it's a natural, low-cost bridge question — "did putting officials on camera change what you understood" — that costs little time and can surface something the book's prose alone didn't.

**Update (audience fit):** the novelty test isn't the only axis that matters — relevance to *this specific audience* is a second, independent dimension, and it changes the ranking. An open-source security audience already lives inside supply-chain risk; a question that connects Sanger's nation-state cyber-conflict expertise to their own domain (XZ Utils as a state-tradecraft-style attack on open-source trust networks, not a government network) scores high on both axes at once — it's novel for Sanger to be asked directly, and it's maximally relevant to the room. That combination outranks the AI question on pure audience fit, even though the AI material is arguably more novel in the abstract, because the AI question could be asked of Sanger at any venue while the open-source question can really only be asked well at this one. First-principles conclusion, revised: structure the interview around two paired priority questions — the "is cyber still the perfect weapon" spine, immediately followed by the open-source/XZ Utils bridge — with the AI question and the Cold War-metaphor pushback as strong secondary material, and the book/documentary folded in as brief, optional color rather than dedicated segments.

## Stress Test (Popper)

Four objections to the initial framing, each addressed below rather than left standing:

1. **"What's changed since 2018" is itself now a canned question.** Sanger has been asked a version of this in every venue for eight years and will likely have a fluent, general answer ready. *Resolution (Seldon, below):* the fix isn't to drop the question but to make it specific and falsifiable — anchor it to his own named comparison ("perfect weapon" vs. the *New Cold Wars* framing) rather than asking generically what's changed, which forces a sharper, less pre-packaged answer.

2. **Is the HBO documentary worth any of the 20 minutes at all?** It's six years old, thoroughly covered in its own press cycle, and arguably a distraction from the more topical AI/*New Cold Wars* material given how scarce the time is. *Resolution:* keep it, but only as a single low-cost bridge question, not a segment — and treat it as expendable first if time runs short.

3. **Risk of conflating "cyber broadly" with "AI-enabled cyber/disinformation specifically."** These are not the same phenomenon, and a sloppy question could let Sanger answer in generalities about cyber threats rather than the AI-specific acceleration he actually flagged in April. *Resolution:* the question as drafted explicitly asks him to make the AI angle concrete ("what does an AI-accelerated version of this look like, and have you already seen one") rather than asking generally "how has cyber changed."

4. **The Cold War framing in *New Cold Wars* shouldn't be accepted uncritically.** Foreign Affairs and other reviewers flagged it as a potential oversimplification of a rivalry that's as much about technological/economic entanglement as classic military standoff. *Resolution:* included directly as one of the three priority questions, framed as a challenge ("does that metaphor hold up, or does it flatten something that's actually different from 1962") rather than accepted at face value — this is the interview's best opportunity for genuine pushback rather than a recap.

5. **Structural objection: is five-to-six questions actually realistic for 20 minutes, or is that still too many?** *Resolution (Seldon, below):* addressed directly with a time-budgeted table rather than left as a vague count.

**Two new objections from adding the open-source angle:**

6. **Overclaiming attribution on XZ Utils.** It would be easy for a question to imply "a nation-state did this" as settled fact, when Ryan's tagging above shows campaign attribution is strong but actor attribution is not formally established. *Resolution:* the open-source bridge question is phrased to describe the tradecraft ("resembles intelligence-service patience and resourcing") without asserting a named sponsor, and explicitly invites Sanger — who has far more attribution-reporting experience than most of the room — to say what he does or doesn't believe about who was behind it, which is itself a legitimate and interesting question rather than something to route around.

7. **Adding a fourth substantive thread (spine, open-source, AI, Cold War metaphor) risks diluting a 20-minute interview into four shallow mini-segments instead of two or three deep ones.** *Resolution (Seldon, below):* rather than treat all four as equal, the time-budgeted table below sequences them by combined novelty-and-audience-fit priority (open-source bridge now ranks directly behind the spine, ahead of the AI question) and makes the two lowest-priority items — book retrospective and the HBO bridge — explicitly the first to compress or drop, so adding one high-value thread doesn't come at the cost of shallow coverage everywhere; it comes at the cost of the two threads that were already marked optional.

## Forecast (Seldon)

How the 20 minutes actually gets spent depends heavily on how Rick opens, and that split is the main source of uncertainty here — not whether Sanger will have interesting things to say (he reliably does).

If Rick opens with the forward-looking synthesis question (is cyber still the perfect weapon, or has AI/great-power rivalry superseded it) rather than chronologically with the book, the range of how much of the 20 minutes reaches 2024–2026 material runs from roughly 10 to 16 minutes, with a median around 13 — because Sanger tends to reach back into book-era examples anyway when illustrating a present-day point, so the older material still surfaces, just as supporting color inside forward-looking answers rather than as its own block.

If Rick instead opens chronologically with the book, that range shifts down substantially — perhaps 4 to 10 minutes reaching the 2024–2026 material, median around 7 — because subject-matter experts asked to retell well-worn history tend to expand rather than compress an answer, and a 20-minute clock leaves little room to recover lost time later. There's a meaningful chance (not the median case, but not negligible) that the AI/*New Cold Wars* material gets cut short or dropped entirely under this ordering if an early answer runs long.

This is reasoned judgment based on how interview time typically gets consumed when a subject has a large back catalog to draw on, not measured data from this specific interview — there's no way to verify it before Thursday. The practical implication is unaffected by exactly where in the range the real outcome lands: front-load the newest material regardless, since every scenario favors that ordering over a chronological one.

**Update:** adding the open-source bridge question doesn't materially change this forecast's shape, but it does change which minutes are most worth protecting. Because it's a question Sanger likely hasn't been asked in this specific form before, there's more uncertainty in how long his answer runs than for the well-rehearsed spine question — the range for that one segment alone is wide, plausibly anywhere from 3 to 6 minutes with a median around 4, since a genuinely novel question can produce either a tight, considered answer or an expansive one as the subject thinks out loud. Given that uncertainty, it's placed second (right after the spine) rather than later, so that if it does run long, it consumes time that was already budgeted toward it rather than crowding out the AI or Cold War-metaphor questions behind it.
