# ELI5: Mayhem, the Cyber Grand Challenge, and DEF CON's First Non-Human Black Badge (2016)

**Question:** Explain, simply, the origin-history report's timeline entry: "2016 (DEF CON 24): DARPA's Cyber Grand Challenge AI system 'Mayhem' won its all-machine competition and then entered the humans-only CTF (finishing last); it received the first Black Badge ever awarded to a non-human entity, later displayed at the Smithsonian."

## Sherlock — What are the facts?

**ELI5 first, then the sourced detail.**

**ELI5:** DARPA — the Pentagon's futuristic research arm — ran a competition to answer a specific question: can a computer program find and fix its own security holes, entirely on its own, faster than a human could? Seven teams built AI systems and let them fight each other automatically: find bugs in unfamiliar code, patch your own weaknesses, and exploit the other systems' weaknesses, all without a human touching the keyboard. The winner, a program called Mayhem (built by a team called ForAllSecure, spun out of Carnegie Mellon), took home $2 million. That part happened in a machine-only bracket. Then, because it was already at DEF CON, the organizers let Mayhem enter the *human* Capture the Flag tournament — the real, top-tier hacking competition humans have been fighting to qualify for since DEF CON's earliest years. Mayhem finished dead last (15th out of 15), but it wasn't a joke performance — at one point it was briefly sitting in 13th, genuinely ahead of two human teams, which is the detail that made people take it seriously rather than laugh it off. DEF CON gave it a Black Badge — the conference's highest honor, normally a hand-made, one-of-a-kind piece of hacker jewelry given to actual human competition winners — making it the first non-human ever to receive one. It's now sitting in the Smithsonian.

**The sourced facts, with names, dates, and figures confirmed:**

- **The Cyber Grand Challenge final was held August 4, 2016, in the Paris Las Vegas Grand Ballroom**, in front of roughly 5,000 attendees, as part of DEF CON 24.
- **Seven autonomous "Cyber Reasoning Systems" competed head-to-head for nearly 10 hours**, in a Capture the Flag-style format: each system had to find vulnerabilities in unfamiliar, custom-built test code, patch its own flaws within *seconds* (compared to the months a human security team typically needs), and probe the other systems for exploitable weaknesses.
- **Mayhem, built by ForAllSecure (a Pittsburgh-based team spun out of Carnegie Mellon), won first place and $2 million.** Second place (Xandra, by team TECHx) took $1 million; third (Mechanical Phish, by team Shellphish) took $750,000.
- **DARPA's program manager, Mike Walker, on why it mattered:** *"I'm enormously gratified that we achieved CGC's primary goal, which was to provide clear proof of principle that machine-speed, scalable cyber defense is indeed possible."*
- **Mayhem then became the first autonomous system to compete in DEF CON's human CTF finals** — a separate, harder, adjacent event from the all-machine DARPA competition. Final result: **15th place out of 15 teams.** But its position wasn't static throughout — on Saturday morning of the competition, it was briefly ranked **13th out of 15**, genuinely outperforming two human teams at that point in the tournament. One of Mayhem's own designers, Alex Rebert, said afterward he was glad it wasn't dead last at that stage.
- **DEF CON awarded Mayhem the first-ever Black Badge given to a non-human entity.** The Black Badge is DEF CON's highest recognition — historically a distinctive, individually crafted piece given to the actual winners of the conference's marquee competitions, and among the hacking community's most prestigious honors.
- **Mayhem is now on physical display at the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History**, in the museum's innovation wing — the actual hardware, lit with the same LEDs it had during the competition, alongside explanatory placards and video footage from the Cyber Grand Challenge and interviews with the ForAllSecure team. Arthur Daemmrich, director of the Smithsonian's Lemelson Center, on why it was chosen for exhibit: it represents "not only an innovative approach to cybersecurity" but also "a symbol of the advancements in artificial intelligence."

## Euclid — What must be fundamentally true?

Two things have to be true for this story to be the landmark it's remembered as, not just a neat demo:

- **The two competitions Mayhem entered are structurally different, and the second one is the part that actually proved something.** Winning the all-machine Cyber Grand Challenge proves a system can out-automate other automated systems — a closed, controlled comparison. Entering the *human* CTF and finishing competitively (briefly 13th, not immediately dead last) is a much harder, open-ended claim: that an autonomous system, with no human in the loop, could hold its own for stretches against people who'd spent years qualifying for DEF CON's most prestigious hacking tournament. The first result is impressive within its own category; the second is the one that made the security community pay attention, because it wasn't a competition designed for a machine to win.
- **DEF CON handing out its highest human-competition honor to a piece of hardware is a deliberate statement about category, not a formality.** A Black Badge has always meant "a human being demonstrated elite hacking skill." Extending that specifically to Mayhem — rather than, say, creating a separate "best AI" award — is DEF CON explicitly saying the machine's *performance* met the same bar the badge has always certified, not that it deserves a lesser, AI-specific consolation prize. That's a stronger claim than "impressive robot," and it's exactly why the honor (and the eventual Smithsonian placement) stuck as a genuinely notable historical marker rather than a novelty.

## Popper — How could we be wrong?

1. **The Smithsonian's own published materials about the exhibit, as directly checked in this pass, do not themselves mention the Black Badge award** — the museum's framing emphasizes the Cyber Grand Challenge win and the broader AI/cybersecurity narrative, not the DEF CON Black Badge specifically. The Black Badge claim is well-corroborated by two independent, non-Smithsonian sources found in this research, but this report can't say the Smithsonian itself cites the badge as part of why Mayhem was selected for display.
2. **This report didn't independently verify the exact wording or criteria DEF CON used when awarding the Black Badge to Mayhem** — it's confirmed as a real, first-of-its-kind award from multiple sources, but this pass didn't locate a first-party DEF CON statement (e.g., from Jeff Moss or the CTF organizers) explaining their specific reasoning for extending the honor to a non-human system.
3. **The "13th out of 15, ahead of two human teams" detail, while sourced, describes a single point-in-time snapshot (Saturday morning) in a multi-day tournament** — it's accurately reported as a genuine mid-competition standing, not the final result, but worth being precise that Mayhem's overall trajectory was a decline from that peak to 15th, not a steady middling performance throughout.

## Tufte — How do we make this clear?

A short table separates the two distinct competitions Mayhem entered, since conflating them is the easiest way to misread this story — "won" applies to one event, "finished last but respectably" applies to the other, and they're often collapsed into a single confusing sentence:

| | DARPA Cyber Grand Challenge (Aug 4, 2016) | DEF CON 24 Human CTF Finals |
|---|---|---|
| **Competitors** | 7 autonomous systems only | 15 teams, all human except Mayhem |
| **Format** | ~10-hour, machine-speed vulnerability discovery, patching, and exploitation | DEF CON's marquee human hacking tournament |
| **Mayhem's result** | **1st place, $2 million** | **15th of 15 (last)** — but briefly 13th at one point, ahead of two human teams |
| **Recognition** | DARPA prize money | First-ever DEF CON Black Badge awarded to a non-human entity |
| **Later legacy** | — | On permanent display at the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History |
