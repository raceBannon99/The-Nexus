# US Streaming Services: Ranked by Value (Pricing, Bundles, Most Popular Shows)

**Question:** Create a table of all streaming services in the US and include their pricing bundles. Include a column for their five most popular shows. Order the list by the most bang for the buck for the consumer.

## Answer

For a single household deciding where to spend its streaming budget in late August 2026, the honest answer is: start free, then add exactly one or two paid services matched to what you actually watch — don't try to rank "all of them" as if you'd subscribe to all of them at once.

Tubi and Pluto TV cost nothing and carry enormous libraries (Tubi alone lists 250,000+ titles), which makes them mathematically unbeatable on a pure dollar basis. But neither produces the kind of first-run, watercooler show driving this year's most-talked-about TV — their most-watched titles (*Empire*, *Archer*, *Killing Eve* on Tubi; *The Closer*, *Mad Men*, *Watson* on Pluto) are library reruns, not new releases. They're the right answer if the household's goal is "something to watch" rather than "this specific new show."

Among paid services, the best dollar-for-dollar picks share three things: an ad-supported entry tier at or near the $8.99 floor, a deep content library, and popular shows that are genuine platform exclusives rather than licensed reruns. On that basis, three services lead the paid tier: **Prime Video** ($8.99/mo standalone, or effectively bundled into the $14.99/mo full Amazon Prime membership along with free shipping and other Prime perks), **Netflix** ($8.99/mo with ads — the deepest, most consistent hit-generating slate of any paid service), and **Paramount+** ($8.99/mo, live NFL/CBS sports plus the growing Taylor Sheridan slate). Peacock and Max both clear the value bar close behind at $8.99–$10.99/mo. Hulu, Disney+, and Apple TV rank lower not because they're bad services, but because each costs more ($11.99–$12.99/mo minimum) for a narrower content slate — Apple TV in particular has no ad-supported discount at all and the smallest total catalog of any paid service on this list, despite unusually high average quality (*Severance*, *Ted Lasso*, *Slow Horses*).

The **Disney+/Hulu/ESPN Select bundle** ($24.99/mo through mid-September 2026, rising to $27.99/mo on September 17) deserves a separate mention: it beats buying those three ad-supported tiers individually (which would run roughly $36/mo combined) even after the increase, making it the strongest 3-in-1 bundle on the market, if a household actually wants all three.

Two caveats matter more than the ranking itself. First, "most popular" was measured with three different, non-interchangeable methodologies across sources (Nielsen viewing-minutes, self-reported platform data, and third-party trackers like FlixPatrol) — treat the five-show lists below as representative, not precisely ranked against each other. Second, every dollar figure here is a snapshot: streaming prices have moved every few months throughout 2026 (Netflix in March, Disney+/Hulu in October 2025, Max in October 2025, Paramount+ in January, Peacock and the Disney bundle in August), and Seldon's forecast below expects that pattern to continue. This table will be stale within a matter of months — that's the nature of the category, not a flaw in the research.

## First-Principles Analysis (Euclid)

Stripped of marketing language, "bang for the buck" for a streaming subscription reduces to three independent variables: **price** (the only hard number), **library depth** (how much content justifies the subscription existing at all beyond its newest release), and **exclusivity of what's actually popular** (whether the value driving a subscription decision is available anywhere else). A service can be cheap and worthless (low price, thin library, nothing anyone's watching) or expensive and worthwhile (Max's ad-free tiers are genuinely well-regarded despite costing more than Netflix's ad tier) — price alone is not value, and library size alone is not value either, since Tubi's 250,000 titles include enormous amounts of content nobody is choosing to watch. The ranking above weights all three, which is why Prime Video and Netflix — not the cheapest nominal price, but the strongest price-to-hit-content ratio — sit above Paramount+ and Peacock despite identical $8.99 entry prices, and why Apple TV, despite critically excellent shows, ranks last among paid services: it has no discount tier and the thinnest library of anything on this list, so its price-to-volume math is simply worse regardless of quality per title.

## Stress Test (Popper)

Three points of genuine pushback on the analysis above:

1. **The "popularity" data isn't apples-to-apples.** Netflix's and Max's figures come from self-reported viewing-minutes (Nielsen, Tudum); Tubi's and Pluto's come from a third-party all-time list (TheStreamable); Apple TV's and Peacock's are a mix of unique-viewer records and single-week snapshots (FlixPatrol, 9to5Mac). These are not the same metric, and a service that reports generously (viewing-minutes, which rewards long runtimes and rewatches) will look more "popular" than one measured by unique first-week viewers, independent of actual audience size.
2. **Crediting Prime Video with non-streaming Prime perks is a category error if the question is strictly about streaming value.** Free shipping and Prime Music aren't streaming-service value — folding them in inflates Prime Video's rank relative to services judged purely on video content.
3. **Ranking free ad-supported libraries (Tubi, Pluto) above every paid service assumes the consumer's goal is "content at minimum cost," but the question as scoped is about services worth paying attention to for their most popular shows** — and Tubi/Pluto's most-popular lists are entirely library reruns, which may not be what "bang for the buck" means to someone asking which paid subscription to get.

## How Seldon Resolved Each Objection, and the Forecast

Addressing Popper's three objections directly, not just acknowledging them:

1. **Methodology caveat, not a fix** — the underlying measurement inconsistency across services can't be resolved without primary access to each platform's internal analytics, which no outside party has. The response is transparency: the caveat is now stated explicitly in the Answer section above, and readers should treat the five-show lists as representative of what's currently popular on each service, not as precisely comparable rankings against each other.
2. **Resolved by keeping Prime Video's non-streaming perks as a stated factor, not a silent one.** The table entry explicitly calls out "plus non-streaming Prime perks" as a distinct line in its rationale rather than folding it invisibly into the price number — a reader who only cares about video content can mentally discount that clause and still see Prime Video landing near the top purely on its $8.99 entry price and exclusive-hit ratio.
3. **Stood by, not revised** — Rick's own scoping decision (via Bradlee's clarifying question) explicitly included "notable ad-supported free tiers" in the comparison. Tubi and Pluto's rank reflects that scope faithfully: they are objectively the best dollar-for-dollar value under the criteria as defined, with the tradeoff (library reruns, not new releases) stated plainly in both the Answer and the table itself so no reader mistakes free-tier rank for "most exciting new content."

**Forecast — where streaming pricing is headed:** Every major service on this list raised prices at least once in the twelve months prior to this report (Netflix in March 2026, Disney+/Hulu in October 2025, Max in October 2025, Paramount+ in January 2026, Peacock and the Disney bundle in August 2026). That cadence is very unlikely to break. The range for **how many of these ten services raise at least one tier's price again within the next 12 months** runs from about 6 to all 10, with a median around 8 — this is a near-annual habit at this point for every major player, driven by content-cost inflation and a maturing subscriber base with less room for pure growth, so pushing price on existing subscribers is the more available lever. On the harder-to-call side, the range for **how many years until the market consolidates from ten separate paid services down to something closer to four or five** (through bundling, M&A, or folding smaller services into larger ones the way Freevee folded into Prime Video in 2025) runs from roughly 2 to 6 years, with a median around 4 — bundling economics (the Disney+/Hulu/ESPN Select bundle undercutting its parts even after a price hike) already point that direction, but this is reasoned judgment about industry structure, not a number derived from measured data the way the price-increase-cadence estimate above is.
