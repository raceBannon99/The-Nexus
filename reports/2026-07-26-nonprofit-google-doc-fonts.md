# Five Fonts for a Nonprofit Business Google Doc

**Question:** Recommend five fonts to use in a business nonprofit Google Doc.

## Alexandria — What do we already know?

Nothing in the artifact library or prior Nexus reports addresses font or document-design choices directly. The one adjacent artifact — the Edward Tufte visualization-principles fact-sheet — covers chart and table design, not typography selection, so it isn't a substitute answer here. ## Sherlock — What are the facts?

Four fact clusters bear directly on the choice:

1. **Licensing is a non-issue.** Every typeface in the Google Fonts catalog — which is what populates Google Docs' "More fonts" picker — ships under the SIL Open Font License (OFL). The OFL permits free use, commercial or not, with no attribution requirement and no per-seat or per-document fee. A nonprofit can standardize on any of these fonts across every document without a licensing review.
2. **Convention in the sector is split, and generational.** Grant-writing guidance still frequently defaults to Times New Roman or Arial at 11–12pt — a holdover from print-and-mail submission norms — while newer brand-guide guidance for nonprofits (re:charity, 501 Commons, Elevation Web) recommends pairing a distinct heading face with a body face, drawn from the modern Google Fonts catalog, and treats Times New Roman as dated rather than neutral.
3. **Accessibility is treated as a first-class requirement, not a nice-to-have, in nonprofit-specific guidance.** Recommendations converge on a short list — Inter, Roboto, Open Sans, Noto Sans, and Atkinson Hyperlegible — chosen for x-height, clear differentiation between easily-confused characters (I/l, 0/O), and reliable rendering at small sizes. Atkinson Hyperlegible in particular was developed by the Braille Institute specifically for low-vision readers. Nonprofit guidance also sets numeric floors: body text no smaller than ~18px on screen, line spacing at 1.5×, weights of 400 or heavier.
4. **General "best Google Docs fonts" lists** (How-To Geek, Typewolf, Jukebox Print) converge on the same handful of names for professional use: Open Sans, Roboto, Lora, Merriweather, Inter — all free, all screen-tested, all available natively.

## Euclid — What must be fundamentally true?

Stripped of brand preference, the constraints that actually govern this choice are:

- **The document lives on a screen first.** Google Docs is read on laptops and phones far more often than printed; a font's screen legibility at 11–12pt matters more than its appearance on paper.
- **The reader is not one person.** A nonprofit business document — grant proposal, board report, donor letter — is read by funders, board members, staff, and sometimes beneficiaries, a group that skews older and includes people with low vision more often than a typical corporate audience does. A font that only works for a 25-year-old designer's eyes is the wrong choice here.
- **Cost and licensing risk must be zero.** A nonprofit has no budget line for font licenses and no legal team to review one. This is automatically satisfied by staying inside the Google Fonts/OFL catalog — it isn't really a differentiator between the five choices below, it's a floor everything must clear.
- **Font choice is read as a proxy for institutional competence.** A funder skimming a proposal reads a cramped, ugly, or overly decorative document as a signal of a disorganized organization, independent of the content's merit. This argues for restraint — one heading face, one body face — over variety.

Given those four constraints, the fundamentally sound shortlist is a mix of maximally safe screen-legible sans fonts, one serif option for documents that want more formality, and one accessibility-first pick that a mission-driven organization has particular reason to reach for:

| # | Font | Type | Best use in this context |
|---|------|------|---------------------------|
| 1 | **Open Sans** | Sans-serif | Default body text — neutral, high legibility, the safest all-purpose choice |
| 2 | **Roboto** | Sans-serif | Alternative body/UI-adjacent text — Google's own workhorse, very reliable cross-device rendering |
| 3 | **Lora** | Serif | Headings or full body in more formal reports (board minutes, annual reports) — reads as substantive without Times New Roman's dated feel |
| 4 | **Atkinson Hyperlegible** | Sans-serif | Any document going to donors, beneficiaries, or a public audience where low-vision accessibility is a real concern — directly mission-aligned for most nonprofits |
| 5 | **Merriweather** | Serif | Cover pages, section headers, or pull quotes — polished and readable at both large and small sizes, pairs cleanly with either sans body font above |

Practical pairing guidance: pick **one** body font (Open Sans, Roboto, or Atkinson Hyperlegible) and, optionally, **one** heading font (Lora or Merriweather) — using all five in one document would defeat the restraint principle above.

## Popper — How could we be wrong?

Three objections worth taking seriously:

1. **Some funders still expect Times New Roman or Arial, full stop.** Certain foundations and government grant portals specify font and size in their submission instructions (a holdover from print/mail norms), and a small number of program officers read anything else as informal. Euclid's list is right for documents the nonprofit controls the formatting of — internal reports, donor letters, its own proposal templates — but wrong to apply blindly to a funder-specified template. Check the RFP first; when a font is mandated, comply, don't substitute.
2. **Atkinson Hyperlegible is distinctive-looking, and distinctiveness cuts both ways.** Its letterforms are deliberately exaggerated to disambiguate similar characters, which is exactly why it works for low-vision readers — but that same distinctiveness means a sophisticated donor or board member unfamiliar with its purpose could read it as unusual or overly casual rather than accessible. This is a real tradeoff, not a reason to drop it: the fix is to use it selectively (public-facing, high-accessibility-need documents) rather than as the universal default, and to be ready to explain the choice if asked.
3. **A recommended list of five invites the opposite of restraint.** Handing someone five names risks exactly the "one-of-everything" clutter Euclid's own principle warns against. The list is a shortlist to choose from, not a palette to use simultaneously.

## Seldon — What is likely to happen next, and how do Popper's objections resolve?

Addressing Popper's three points directly:

- **On funder-mandated fonts:** stands as written — no revision needed. Euclid's shortlist is for organization-controlled documents; RFP-specified formatting always overrides it. This isn't really a forecasting question, it's a scope boundary.
- **On Atkinson Hyperlegible reading as unusual:** stands, with the usage guidance already attached (selective use, public/accessibility-relevant documents) rather than a universal default. No change to the shortlist itself.
- **On the five-font list inviting clutter:** the report now states the pairing guidance explicitly (one body, optionally one heading) directly under the table above, which resolves the concern without shrinking the list — a five-item shortlist is still useful precisely because it lets an organization pick per-document-type rather than being locked to one pair everywhere.

One forward-looking read: nonprofit-sector adoption of accessibility-first typography (the Atkinson Hyperlegible line of thinking) is likely to keep strengthening rather than stay a niche practice, driven less by aesthetic trends than by two concrete pressures — Department of Justice ADA Title II digital-accessibility rules that already apply to state/local government sites and are increasingly referenced as a baseline expectation for organizations that receive public funding, and donor-side expectations catching up to what's already normal in web design. The range for when "accessible-by-default" typography becomes a standard, not-remarked-upon expectation across the U.S. nonprofit sector (rather than a differentiator a handful of orgs practice) runs from roughly 2 to 6 years out, with a median around 4 years. This is reasoned judgment from the direction of regulatory and sector pressure, not a measured trend line — there's no dataset tracking nonprofit font choices over time to anchor it more tightly.
