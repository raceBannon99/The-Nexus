# Five Fonts for a Nonprofit Business Google Doc

**Question:** Recommend five fonts to use in a business nonprofit Google Doc.

## Sherlock — What are the facts?

Five fact clusters bear directly on the choice:

1. **Licensing is a non-issue.** Every typeface in the Google Fonts catalog — including the fonts built into Google Docs' default picker and the larger set behind "More fonts" — ships under the SIL Open Font License (OFL). The OFL permits free use, commercial or not, with no attribution requirement and no per-seat or per-document fee.
2. **The default Google Docs font dropdown is larger than most write-ups suggest, and it's worth verifying directly rather than trusting secondhand blog lists — several sources checked for the original version of this report gave inconsistent or incomplete answers.** Opening a blank Google Doc and reading the font dropdown directly (no "More fonts" click) shows the built-in list is: Allerta Stencil, Amatic SC, Arial, Calibri, Caveat, Comfortaa, Comic Sans MS, Courier New, EB Garamond, Georgia, Gill Sans, Helvetica Neue, Impact, Lexend, Lobster, Lora, Merriweather, Montserrat, Nunito, Oswald, Pacifico, Playfair Display, Plus Jakarta Sans, Roboto (plus Roboto Mono and Roboto Serif), Ruslan Display, Short Stack, Spectral, Stardos Stencil, Times New Roman, Trebuchet MS, and Verdana — 33 fonts, mixing classic system fonts with a meaningful slice of the Google Fonts catalog. Notably, **Open Sans, Inter, Noto Sans, and Atkinson Hyperlegible are not in this list** — each requires "More fonts," which the "install nothing" constraint rules out.
3. **Convention in the sector is split, and generational.** Grant-writing guidance still frequently defaults to Times New Roman or Arial at 11–12pt — a holdover from print-and-mail submission norms — while newer brand-guide guidance for nonprofits (re:charity, 501 Commons, Elevation Web) recommends pairing a distinct heading face with a body face, and treats Times New Roman as dated rather than neutral. Both ends of that split are satisfied by the built-in list: Times New Roman and Arial are right there by default, as are more modern picks.
4. **Accessibility is treated as a first-class requirement, not a nice-to-have, in nonprofit-specific guidance** — but the strongest-credentialed accessibility picks (Atkinson Hyperlegible, Noto Sans, Open Sans) all sit behind "More fonts" and are out of scope under this constraint. One built-in exception: **Lexend** is in the default list and carries real research backing — designed by Dr. Bonnie Shaver-Troup (The Lexend Project) around reading-science principles (wide letter spacing, expanded horizontal proportions, reduced visual crowding) and hosted on Google Fonts since 2018; a doctoral study found it improved oral reading fluency in second-grade readers, and Google's own design team has written about broader reading-speed gains across proficient and struggling readers alike.
5. **General "best Google Docs fonts" lists** (How-To Geek, Typewolf, Jukebox Print) converge on Open Sans, Roboto, Lora, Merriweather, and Inter as professional picks — but only Roboto, Lora, and Merriweather from that set are actually built in without "More fonts."

## Euclid — What must be fundamentally true?

Stripped of brand preference, the constraints that actually govern this choice are:

- **The document lives on a screen first.** Google Docs is read on laptops and phones far more often than printed; a font's screen legibility at 11–12pt matters more than its appearance on paper.
- **The reader is not one person.** A nonprofit business document — grant proposal, board report, donor letter — is read by funders, board members, staff, and sometimes beneficiaries, a group that skews older and includes people with low vision more often than a typical corporate audience does. A font that only works for a 25-year-old designer's eyes is the wrong choice here.
- **Cost and licensing risk must be zero, and now so must setup friction.** Rick's constraint adds a fifth: the font has to be usable the moment the document is opened, with no "More fonts" detour. This isn't just convenience — it also means every future editor of the document, on any account, sees the same fonts with zero setup, which matters more for an organization than for a single writer.
- **Font choice is read as a proxy for institutional competence.** A funder skimming a proposal reads a cramped, ugly, or overly decorative document as a signal of a disorganized organization, independent of the content's merit. This argues for restraint — one heading face, one body face — over variety.

Given those constraints, and restricted to the 33-font built-in list Sherlock verified directly in the product, the fundamentally sound shortlist is:

| # | Font | Type | Best use in this context |
|---|------|------|---------------------------|
| 1 | **Calibri** | Sans-serif | Default body text — neutral, modern business-standard (also Microsoft Office's default), the safest all-purpose choice that needs no setup |
| 2 | **Roboto** | Sans-serif | Alternative body/UI-adjacent text — Google's own workhorse, very reliable cross-device rendering |
| 3 | **Lora** | Serif | Headings or full body in more formal reports (board minutes, annual reports) — reads as substantive without Times New Roman's dated feel |
| 4 | **Lexend** | Sans-serif | Any document going to donors, beneficiaries, or a public audience where reading ease matters — research-backed for reading fluency, and unlike Atkinson Hyperlegible it's built into Google Docs by default |
| 5 | **Merriweather** | Serif | Cover pages, section headers, or pull quotes — polished and readable at both large and small sizes, pairs cleanly with either sans body font above |

Two of the five changed from the original version of this report: Open Sans → **Calibri**, and Atkinson Hyperlegible → **Lexend** — both because the originals require "More fonts." Roboto, Lora, and Merriweather were already built-in and are unchanged.

Practical pairing guidance: pick **one** body font (Calibri, Roboto, or Lexend) and, optionally, **one** heading font (Lora or Merriweather) — using all five in one document would defeat the restraint principle above.

## Popper — How could we be wrong?

**Re-reviewed for the update pass**, since swapping in Lexend touches a conclusion originally raised against Atkinson Hyperlegible. Three objections worth taking seriously:

1. **Some funders still expect Times New Roman or Arial, full stop.** Certain foundations and government grant portals specify font and size in their submission instructions (a holdover from print/mail norms), and a small number of program officers read anything else as informal. Euclid's list is right for documents the nonprofit controls the formatting of — internal reports, donor letters, its own proposal templates — but wrong to apply blindly to a funder-specified template. Check the RFP first; when a font is mandated, comply, don't substitute. **This objection is now easier to satisfy than in the original report**: Times New Roman and Arial are themselves in the built-in list, so complying with a mandated font and staying inside the "install nothing" constraint are no longer in tension.
2. **Does Lexend inherit Atkinson Hyperlegible's "reads as unusual" problem?** No — this objection substantially weakens on re-review rather than needing the same workaround. Atkinson Hyperlegible's letterforms are deliberately exaggerated to disambiguate similar characters, which is visually distinctive by design. Lexend's reading-fluency gains instead come from spacing and proportion, not unusual letterforms — at a glance it reads as an ordinary, slightly wide humanist sans, not as a "special" font. A donor or board member is unlikely to consciously notice anything unusual about it. The original guidance (use it selectively, be ready to explain the choice) is now more caution than the swap actually requires, but there's no harm in keeping it.
3. **A recommended list of five invites the opposite of restraint.** Handing someone five names risks exactly the "one-of-everything" clutter Euclid's own principle warns against. The list is a shortlist to choose from, not a palette to use simultaneously. Unchanged by the update.

## Seldon — What is likely to happen next, and how do Popper's objections resolve?

Addressing Popper's three points directly:

- **On funder-mandated fonts:** stands as written, and now trivially satisfied — Euclid's shortlist is for organization-controlled documents; RFP-specified formatting always overrides it, and both commonly-mandated fonts (Times New Roman, Arial) are built-in anyway.
- **On Lexend's distinctiveness:** resolved favorably on re-review — it doesn't carry Atkinson Hyperlegible's "reads as unusual" risk, so no special usage caveat is really needed, though keeping it doesn't hurt.
- **On the five-font list inviting clutter:** unchanged — the pairing guidance under the table (one body, optionally one heading) resolves this without shrinking the list.

**Revised forward-looking read.** The original forecast — that nonprofit-sector adoption of accessibility-first typography keeps strengthening, driven by ADA Title II digital-accessibility rules and donor expectations catching up to web-design norms — still holds; that trend isn't about any single font. But the "install nothing" constraint changes where this report's own recommendation sits on that trend line: Lexend is a real, if modest, step in that direction, not the strongest one available. Organizations with a genuinely high concentration of low-vision readers in their audience — many social-service and aging-focused nonprofits, for instance — have reason to spend the one extra click on "More fonts" for Atkinson Hyperlegible or Noto Sans despite this report's default-only framing, since both are still free and still take seconds to add. The range for when accessible-by-default typography becomes a standard, unremarked expectation across the U.S. nonprofit sector runs from roughly 2 to 6 years out, with a median around 4 years — unchanged from the original estimate, since the underlying regulatory and sector pressure driving it doesn't depend on which specific font a given organization picks today. This remains reasoned judgment, not a measured trend line — there's no dataset tracking nonprofit font choices over time to anchor it more tightly.
